# Financial Statements and Accounting Systems

Financial accounting measures, classifies, summarizes, and communicates quantifiable economic activity to external users.

The accounting system is designed to:

- record transactions systematically;
- automate processing;
- classify and summarize activity;
- preserve an audit trail.

---

# Entity Concept

An accounting entity is treated separately from:

- owners;
- lenders;
- customers;
- suppliers;
- related entities.

This separation matters even when the legal form does not fully separate owners from the business.

---

# Monetary Unit

Accounting needs a common denominator.

Amounts are therefore measured in a common currency.

This creates comparability but also means non-monetary information can be economically important without appearing directly in the statements.

---

# Going Concern

Accounting generally assumes the business will continue normal operations rather than liquidate.

That assumption affects how assets such as PP&E are measured and interpreted.

---

# Balance Sheet

`Assets = Liabilities + Equity`

Better conceptual form:

`Equity = Assets − Liabilities`

Equity is the residual interest of shareholders.

## Assets

Resources that:

- provide probable future benefit;
- are controlled by the entity;
- arise from past events;
- can be measured with sufficient reliability.

## Liabilities

Obligations to:

- pay cash;
- transfer assets;
- provide services.

---

# Income Statement

Income captures changes in shareholder wealth from business activity rather than owner contributions/distributions.

## Revenues

Gross increases in equity from primary activities.

## Expenses

Gross decreases in equity from resources consumed in primary activities.

## Gains / Losses

Net changes in equity from secondary activities.

---

# Contributions and Distributions

Shareholder contributions:

- increase equity;
- are not revenues.

Dividends and buybacks:

- reduce equity;
- are not expenses.

This prevents financing flows from being confused with operating performance.

---

# Double Entry

Every transaction has two equal and opposing aspects.

A cash receipt may reflect:

- revenue;
- borrowing;
- shareholder contribution;
- collection of receivables;
- advance receipt;
- asset sale.

Recording only the cash movement loses the economic cause.

Double-entry bookkeeping preserves both.

---

# Debits and Credits

Basic balance rules:

- asset increases → debit;
- asset decreases → credit;
- liability/equity increases → credit;
- liability/equity decreases → debit.

Contra accounts reverse the normal direction of the related account.

---

# My Take

The most useful way to think about double entry is not "debits and credits."

It is:

> **Every business event has both an economic cause and an economic effect.**

The accounting system forces both to remain visible.
