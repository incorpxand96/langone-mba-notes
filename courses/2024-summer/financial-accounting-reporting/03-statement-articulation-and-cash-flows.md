# Statement Articulation and Cash Flows

The statements are one connected system.

**Articulation** describes the equations linking them.

---

# Basic Articulation

## Cash

`Beginning Cash + Net Cash Flow = Ending Cash`

## Equity

`Beginning Equity + Change in Equity = Ending Equity`

For a simple retained-earnings structure:

`Beginning Retained Earnings + Net Income − Dividends = Ending Retained Earnings`

---

# Cash Flow Classification

## Operating

Cash effects of operating activity.

## Investing

Examples:

- PP&E purchase;
- PP&E sale;
- acquisition/disposition;
- investments.

## Financing

Examples:

- borrowing;
- debt repayment;
- shareholder contribution;
- dividends;
- buybacks.

---

# Direct vs. Indirect Operating Cash Flow

The direct method lists actual operating receipts and payments.

The indirect method starts with net income and reconciles it to operating cash flow.

This reconciliation exists because:

> **income flows and cash flows differ in timing and classification.**

---

# Core Balance-Sheet Logic

From:

`Assets = Liabilities + Equity`

split cash from non-cash assets:

`Cash + Non-Cash Assets = Liabilities + Equity`

Rearrange:

`Cash = − Non-Cash Assets + Liabilities + Equity`

Therefore changes in cash can be derived from changes in the rest of the balance sheet.

---

# Common Operating Adjustments

## Receivables

If receivables increase:

- revenue exceeds receipts;
- subtract the increase from net income to derive operating cash flow.

## Inventory / Prepayments

If operating assets increase:

- cash has been spent without equivalent current expense;
- subtract the increase.

## Payables / Deferred Revenue

If operating liabilities increase:

- expense/revenue recognition differs from payment/receipt timing;
- add the increase when reconciling to operating cash flow.

---

# Depreciation

Depreciation reduces:

- income;
- net PP&E.

It does **not** itself create or consume current-period cash.

The original PP&E purchase is an investing cash flow.

Therefore depreciation is added back in the indirect operating cash-flow reconciliation.

Wrong explanation:

> "Depreciation is a source of cash."

Better explanation:

> **Depreciation is a non-cash current-period expense arising from an earlier investing outflow.**

---

# Non-Operating Changes Matter

A change in a balance-sheet account does not always equal the cash-flow adjustment.

Example:

receivables may change because of:

- operating collections;
- an acquisition;
- foreign currency translation.

The analyst must isolate the operating component.

---

# My Take

The indirect cash flow statement is essentially a map from:

> **accounting performance**

to:

> **cash consequences**

through the balance sheet.
