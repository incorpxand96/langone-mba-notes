# Arbitrage and Market Efficiency

## Theoretical Arbitrage

A pure arbitrage is a trading strategy with:

- no net initial investment;
- non-negative cash flows in all states;
- a positive cash flow in at least one state.

This is stronger than colloquial Wall Street uses of "arbitrage," which may involve risky convergence trades.

---

# No-Arbitrage Condition

If a pure arbitrage exists, traders should exploit it aggressively.

Competitive trading should remove the price discrepancy.

---

# Law of One Price

> **Two securities with identical payoffs must have identical prices.**

Extension:

If a portfolio replicates a security's payoff, the security should equal the cost of the replicating portfolio.

---

# Replicating Portfolio

A replicating portfolio creates the same state-contingent payoff as another security.

This concept appears throughout finance:

- coupon bonds;
- forward rates;
- options;
- synthetic securities.

---

# Real-World Limits to Arbitrage

Apparent mispricing can persist because of:

- transaction costs;
- short-sale constraints;
- leverage costs;
- liquidity;
- basis risk;
- limited investor horizons.

A trade can be economically correct and still fail if the mispricing worsens before converging.

---

# Market Efficiency

Market efficiency means:

> **prices reflect available information sufficiently well that abnormal risk-adjusted profits are difficult to earn systematically.**

It does not mean:

- prices are always correct;
- nobody ever beats the market;
- good news always causes a price increase.

The relevant question is whether the news was already expected and priced.

---

# Forms of Efficiency

## Weak Form

Prices reflect past price information.

## Semi-Strong Form

Prices reflect publicly available information.

Examples:

- earnings releases;
- dividends;
- mergers;
- stock splits.

## Strong Form

Prices reflect all information, including private information.

This is the strongest and least realistic version.

---

# Anomalies

Examples discussed include:

- momentum;
- post-earnings-announcement drift;
- post-IPO drift;
- small-firm / January effects.

Possible explanations include:

- information-processing costs;
- trading costs;
- taxation;
- behavioral underreaction/overreaction.

---

# My Take

Market efficiency is best treated as a competitive benchmark.

The burden of proof belongs to the claim that a repeatable abnormal return exists after:

- risk;
- costs;
- implementation constraints

are properly accounted for.
