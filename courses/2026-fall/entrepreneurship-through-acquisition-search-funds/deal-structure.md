# Deal Structure in ETA

In a small-business acquisition, **price is only one part of the deal**.

Two buyers can agree to the same enterprise value and still propose transactions with very different economics, risk, cash flow, and incentives.

The key lesson is:

> **Purchase price does not tell you how much cash the seller receives at close, who bears the downside, or how the business will be financed after acquisition.**

Deal structure determines those things.

---

## 1. Price Is Not Structure

The enterprise value answers:

> **What value are we placing on the business?**

Deal structure answers:

> **Who provides the money, when does the seller receive it, what obligations remain after closing, and who participates in future upside or downside?**

Important structural questions include:

- How much cash does the seller receive at close?
- How much senior debt does the business carry?
- Is part of the purchase price financed by the seller?
- Does the seller retain equity?
- Is any payment contingent on future performance?
- How much investor equity is required?
- What does the seller do after closing?
- What cash obligations does the company face after acquisition?

The structure can therefore matter as much as — and sometimes more than — the headline valuation.

---

# 2. The Five Primary Financing Levers

The course frames acquisition financing through five major levers.

## 1. Bank / Acquisition Debt

Third-party financing, which may include SBA, conventional, or mezzanine debt.

The class materials note that debt can often represent a substantial portion of deal value.

### Advantages

- reduces the amount of equity required;
- allows the buyer and equity investors to retain more ownership; and
- can improve equity returns when the company performs well.

### Risks

- creates mandatory debt service;
- reduces margin for error;
- can make a modest operating decline much more dangerous; and
- can constrain the company's ability to invest after closing.

Debt increases returns **and** fragility.

---

## 2. Seller Note

The seller finances a portion of the purchase price.

Instead of receiving all consideration at closing, the seller becomes a lender to the buyer or acquisition entity and is repaid over time.

Seller notes are generally junior to senior acquisition debt in the course framework.

A seller note can reduce cash required at close, but it is still **debt**. It creates a future repayment obligation.

See [Seller Financing](seller-financing.md) for a deeper treatment.

---

## 3. Investor Equity

Capital contributed by the buyer and outside investors in exchange for ownership.

Equity has no fixed repayment schedule like debt, but it dilutes the buyer's ownership and gives investors a claim on the company's future value.

Equity investors may also have expectations around:

- governance;
- board participation;
- return thresholds;
- distributions; and
- eventual exit.

The amount of equity required depends partly on how much debt, seller financing, rollover, and contingent consideration can reasonably support the transaction.

---

## 4. Seller Equity Rollover

The seller keeps an ownership stake in the company after closing.

Instead of receiving that portion of value in cash, the seller participates in the future upside and downside of the continuing business.

This can be valuable because it:

- reduces cash required at close;
- signals seller confidence;
- preserves some alignment between seller and buyer; and
- can make the seller economically interested in a successful transition.

But rollover equity also means the seller remains on the cap table, which can create governance and relationship considerations.

---

## 5. Earn-Out

An earn-out makes some portion of the seller's future payment contingent on post-close performance.

The course materials describe earn-outs as commonly tied to performance metrics such as revenue.

Earn-outs can help bridge disagreements about future performance because the buyer pays more if the optimistic case actually occurs.

However, they can also create post-closing conflict if the parties disagree over:

- measurement;
- operating decisions;
- whether targets were achieved; or
- whether the buyer managed the company in a way that affected the payout.

---

# 3. Do Not Confuse the Instruments

These tools can all reduce cash at close, but they allocate risk differently.

| Instrument | Debt or Equity? | Fixed Payment? | Seller Shares Future Upside? | Main Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Senior debt | Debt | Yes | No | Finance purchase |
| Seller note | Debt | Yes | No | Defer part of seller payment |
| Investor equity | Equity | No fixed repayment | Yes | Fund acquisition / absorb risk |
| Seller rollover | Equity | No fixed repayment | Yes | Align seller with future company |
| Earn-out | Contingent consideration | Only if target achieved | Indirectly | Bridge uncertainty about future performance |

A **seller note is not an earn-out**.

A **seller note is not a seller rollover**.

Those distinctions matter because the post-close cash flow and incentives are very different.

---

# 4. Cash at Close

The seller often cares intensely about **cash at close**, not just headline enterprise value.

Consider an illustrative $10 million acquisition:

| Source / Consideration | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Bank debt | $4M |
| Seller note | $2M |
| Investor equity | $1M |
| Seller equity rollover | $2M |
| Earn-out | $1M |
| **Total value** | **$10M** |

Only part of that value is necessarily received by the seller immediately in cash.

That creates room for negotiation.

Two offers with the same headline valuation can have very different:

- cash-at-close amounts;
- certainty of payment;
- seller risk;
- buyer risk; and
- post-close cash burden.

---

# 5. Sources and Uses

A sources-and-uses table forces the buyer to explain where every dollar comes from and where every dollar goes.

## Sources

Typical sources include:

- senior debt;
- seller financing;
- investor equity; and
- buyer equity.

## Uses

Typical uses include:

- cash paid to the seller;
- repayment or treatment of seller financing;
- transaction expenses;
- lender fees;
- diligence costs; and
- additional cash placed on the company's balance sheet.

A transaction is not financed merely because the purchase price is covered.

The buyer must also fund the costs of **getting the deal closed and leaving the business adequately capitalized afterward**.

---

# 6. Deal Structure Is Risk Allocation

The most useful way to think about structure is not as financial engineering, but as **risk allocation**.

Different instruments answer different questions.

### If the seller and buyer disagree about future performance

Possible tool:

- earn-out.

### If the seller wants a higher headline price but the buyer lacks enough cash at close

Possible tools:

- seller note;
- seller rollover; or
- additional equity.

### If the buyer wants the seller economically aligned with the transition

Possible tool:

- seller rollover.

### If the company cannot safely service more debt

Possible response:

- reduce debt;
- increase equity;
- lower the purchase price; or
- use contingent rather than fixed consideration.

The right structure should fit the risk being addressed.

---

# 7. Structure Changes the Post-Acquisition Company

The company does not emerge from closing unchanged.

Before acquisition, it may have:

- no acquisition debt;
- the founder actively involved;
- substantial cash on the balance sheet; and
- no obligation to pay a seller note.

After acquisition, it may have:

- bank debt;
- seller debt;
- transaction fees;
- new investor expectations;
- lower liquidity;
- a departing founder; and
- new management costs.

Therefore the buyer should ask:

> **Can the business still operate comfortably after imposing the acquisition structure on it?**

This is why debt-service coverage and downside modeling matter.

---

# 8. Incentives Matter

Deal structure affects behavior after closing.

A seller who rolls equity remains exposed to the company's future performance.

A seller who holds only a note is primarily a creditor.

A seller with an earn-out may care intensely about the metric used to calculate the earn-out.

These incentives can influence:

- transition support;
- customer introductions;
- employee retention;
- knowledge transfer; and
- conflict after closing.

The structure should therefore be evaluated not just mathematically, but behaviorally.

---

# 9. Negotiating With Multiple Parties

An acquisition typically involves at least three major constituencies:

1. **The seller**
2. **Equity investors**
3. **The lender**

Their goals can conflict.

The seller may want:

- maximum cash at close;
- a high valuation;
- limited post-close risk;
- employee protection;
- legacy preservation; or
- a particular transition role.

The lender may care about:

- leverage;
- collateral;
- debt-service coverage;
- subordination;
- covenants; and
- downside protection.

Equity investors may care about:

- entry valuation;
- governance;
- ownership;
- downside protection; and
- expected returns.

A structure has to work across all three groups.

---

# 10. Understand the Counterparty Before Negotiating

A major class theme is to understand what the other side actually values.

Not every seller is maximizing one number.

A seller may care about:

- legacy;
- employees;
- speed;
- certainty;
- continued ownership;
- retirement;
- reputation; or
- who will operate the company.

If the buyer understands these priorities, concessions can be designed more intelligently.

Something inexpensive to the buyer may be highly valuable to the seller, and vice versa.

---

# 11. Seller Relationships Continue After Closing

Most ETA transactions are not purely transactional.

The seller may still be needed for:

- transition;
- customer introductions;
- supplier relationships;
- employee reassurance;
- technical knowledge; or
- institutional history.

That means a buyer should resist the temptation to "win" every term.

An aggressively optimized deal can destroy the relationship needed to make the acquisition successful.

This is especially relevant to:

- earn-outs;
- seller notes;
- transition compensation; and
- rollover equity.

---

# 12. Worked Example: Why Structure Changed an Attractive Deal

A class example involved an online-auction software business.

The buyer originally contemplated a deal that included approximately **$2 million of seller equity rollover**.

The seller later countered by replacing that rollover with approximately **$2 million of seller financing**.

At first glance, both structures defer $2 million of cash consideration.

Economically, however, they are very different.

## With Seller Rollover

The seller:

- remains an equity owner;
- shares future upside and downside;
- has continued incentive to help the company succeed; and
- does not create the same fixed debt-service obligation.

## With Seller Financing

The seller:

- becomes a creditor;
- expects repayment regardless of equity performance;
- no longer shares the same downside; and
- creates additional post-close cash obligations.

The class example ultimately concluded that the change materially tightened cash flow and weakened seller alignment.

The lesson is:

> **Two instruments with the same face value can create completely different post-close economics.**

---

# 13. Evaluating a Proposed Structure

Before accepting a structure, I would ask:

## Cash Flow

- What are mandatory annual principal and interest payments?
- How much free cash remains after debt service?
- What happens if EBITDA declines?
- Are there balloon payments?
- Does the business still have enough liquidity to operate?

## Incentives

- Who benefits if the company performs well?
- Who bears the downside?
- Is the seller still motivated to help?
- Are incentives likely to create conflict?

## Control

- Who owns the company after closing?
- Does the seller retain governance rights?
- What rights do investors receive?
- Are there board or consent requirements?

## Risk

- Which party is taking the risk around uncertain future performance?
- Could some fixed consideration become contingent?
- Is the buyer paying today for growth that has not yet occurred?

## Flexibility

- Can the company invest after closing?
- Can it survive an unexpected downturn?
- Is refinancing required for the structure to work?

---

# 14. My Deal-Structure Principles

1. **Price and structure must be evaluated together.**
2. **Cash at close is not the same thing as enterprise value.**
3. **Every financing source changes either ownership, cash flow, risk, or incentives.**
4. **Debt magnifies both equity returns and downside risk.**
5. **Seller notes defer payment but create fixed obligations.**
6. **Seller rollover preserves alignment in a way seller debt does not.**
7. **Earn-outs are tools for uncertainty, not ordinary financing.**
8. **Structure should address the actual risk being negotiated.**
9. **Model the company after the acquisition structure is imposed on it.**
10. **Leave a margin of safety for operating mistakes and downside scenarios.**
11. **Understand what the seller, lender, and equity investors each care about.**
12. **Do not optimize terms so aggressively that the post-close relationship becomes dysfunctional.**
