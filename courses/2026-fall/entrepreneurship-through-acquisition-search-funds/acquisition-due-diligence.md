# Acquisition Due Diligence

Due diligence in ETA is not a box-checking exercise.

The goal is to determine whether the business I thought I was buying is actually the business that exists, whether the investment thesis still holds, and what changes once I become the owner.

The most useful framework from class is:

1. **What am I buying?** — Understand the historical machine.
2. **What must remain true once I buy the company?**
3. **How does my buying the company change the business?**

Everything else in diligence should flow from those three questions.

---

## 1. Diligence Is Thesis Verification

By the time a buyer reaches an LOI, there is already an investment thesis.

That thesis usually contains assumptions about:

- the durability of revenue;
- normalized earnings;
- customer retention;
- supplier relationships;
- competitive position;
- owner replacement;
- employee retention;
- working-capital requirements;
- capital expenditures;
- industry growth;
- opportunities for operational improvement; and
- the company's ability to support the proposed capital structure.

Diligence is the process of testing those assumptions.

The objective is not to achieve certainty. No acquisition offers 100% certainty.

The objective is to develop a **clear perspective on the major issues**, quantify downside where possible, and decide whether each issue:

- validates the thesis;
- requires a price adjustment;
- requires a change in deal structure;
- requires contractual protection;
- requires a specific post-close plan; or
- makes the buyer walk away.

---

# 2. Phase 1: Preliminary Due Diligence

Preliminary diligence should be narrow, fast, and focused on the issues capable of killing the deal.

The central question is:

> **What must be true, or I walk away?**

This phase should be completed before spending heavily on accountants, lawyers, consultants, or exhaustive confirmatory work.

## Goals of Preliminary Diligence

### Validate the Investment Thesis

Test the few assumptions that make the business worth buying.

Examples:

- Is revenue actually recurring or highly repeatable?
- Is normalized EBITDA close to what the seller claims?
- Are the largest customers likely to remain?
- Will critical supplier relationships survive a change of control?
- Can the owner actually be replaced?
- Does the company generate enough cash to support the contemplated debt?

### Stress-Test Key Value Drivers

Identify the variables that matter most to value.

If a relatively small change in one assumption destroys the returns or debt-service coverage, that assumption deserves early scrutiny.

### Compare Value to Price

A good business can still be a bad deal.

Preliminary diligence should answer whether the quality and risks of the company justify:

- the headline valuation;
- the EBITDA multiple;
- the amount of leverage;
- seller financing;
- equity required; and
- the buyer's expected return.

### Identify Deal Breakers

Front-load questions where an adverse answer would end the process.

Examples:

- key supplier will not approve the ownership change;
- reported cash flow cannot be reconciled;
- largest customer plans to leave;
- critical IP is not owned by the company;
- business cannot support acquisition debt;
- seller's role cannot realistically be replaced.

The earlier these are tested, the less time and money is wasted.

---

# 3. Phase 2: Confirmatory Due Diligence

If preliminary diligence supports the thesis, the process broadens.

Confirmatory diligence asks:

> **What remains to be verified, and what remedy does each issue require?**

The buyer should continue asking whether there is any reason to walk away, but many issues at this stage are resolved through:

- a price adjustment;
- an earn-out;
- seller financing;
- escrow;
- retention arrangements;
- representations and warranties;
- covenants;
- indemnification;
- changes to working-capital mechanics; or
- a specific post-close operating plan.

Confirmatory diligence should not become mechanical just because the buyer has already won the LOI.

Sunk-cost bias becomes increasingly dangerous as legal and accounting bills accumulate.

---

# 4. Business Due Diligence

Business diligence tests whether the economic machine actually works the way the buyer believes it does.

## Revenue Quality

Questions to answer:

- What percentage of revenue is recurring, contracted, repeat, or project-based?
- How frequently do customers repurchase?
- What is retention by cohort?
- Are there cancellations, churn, or dormant accounts hidden by aggregate growth?
- How concentrated are customers?
- How much revenue depends directly on the owner?
- What is the pipeline, and how reliable is it?
- Are revenue-recognition practices reasonable?

Useful cuts of the data include:

- customer;
- product or SKU;
- geography;
- channel;
- month;
- salesperson;
- customer cohort; and
- gross margin.

A business can show stable total revenue while losing its best customers and replacing them with lower-quality revenue.

## Customer Diligence

Customer concentration should be evaluated both quantitatively and qualitatively.

For material customers, understand:

- length of relationship;
- contract terms;
- renewal mechanics;
- purchasing frequency;
- profitability;
- switching costs;
- relationship owner;
- competitive alternatives; and
- likely reaction to a change of ownership.

Where appropriate, customer calls can test whether the relationship exists with the **company** or primarily with the **seller**.

## Supplier and Partner Diligence

Supplier concentration can be just as dangerous as customer concentration.

Questions include:

- Who are the critical vendors?
- What percentage of inputs or gross profit depends on each?
- Are contracts written?
- Are prices and rebates documented?
- Are agreements assignable?
- Are there change-of-control clauses?
- Can a supplier terminate or renegotiate?
- Could the supplier go direct to the company's customers?
- How important is the company to the supplier?

The key question is not simply whether a relationship has historically been strong.

It is:

> **Will the relationship remain economically and operationally intact after closing?**

## Competitive Position

Understand why the company wins.

Test claims regarding:

- proprietary processes;
- brand;
- reputation;
- switching costs;
- licenses or approvals;
- distribution rights;
- embedded integrations;
- network effects;
- scale;
- location;
- intellectual property; and
- domain expertise.

Ask what a new competitor would find hardest to replicate.

## Market and Industry

The market analysis should be internally consistent.

Common problems include:

- using a narrow market definition to claim leadership;
- switching to a much broader definition when discussing future growth;
- extrapolating temporary growth;
- relying heavily on management projections; and
- ignoring technological or regulatory disruption.

The buyer should understand:

- historical industry growth;
- cyclicality;
- fragmentation;
- regulatory exposure;
- disruption risk;
- competitive entry; and
- realistic sources of future demand.

# 5. Quality of Earnings / Financial Due Diligence

A Quality of Earnings, or **QoE**, is generally performed by an accounting firm.

Its purpose is broader than verifying arithmetic.

It should help determine the **economic earnings power of the company**.

## Normalized Earnings

The buyer wants to know what EBITDA or cash flow should reasonably exist under new ownership.

Typical areas of scrutiny include:

- owner compensation;
- family-member compensation;
- discretionary expenses;
- one-time expenses;
- one-time revenue;
- unusual bonuses;
- legal or professional fees;
- rent adjustments;
- related-party expenses;
- deferred maintenance;
- non-recurring COVID effects; and
- seller-proposed add-backs.

A useful discipline from class is simple:

> **Sort expenses from largest to smallest and work through them.**

Large categories deserve attention before immaterial ones.

## Proof of Cash

Reported earnings should ultimately connect to cash.

Questions include:

- Do bank statements support reported collections?
- Do tax returns broadly reconcile with the income statement?
- Are accounts receivable collectible?
- Are there unexplained differences between cash and reported revenue?
- Are deposits, refunds, or chargebacks treated appropriately?

## Revenue and Profitability Analysis

Break performance down by:

- customer;
- product;
- SKU;
- channel;
- geography; and
- period.

This can reveal that seemingly strong aggregate margins depend on a small subset of customers or products.

## Working Capital

Working capital is not a technical afterthought.

It determines how much capital the buyer needs to keep the company operating after closing.

Review:

- accounts receivable;
- accounts payable;
- inventory;
- deferred revenue;
- accrued expenses;
- seasonality; and
- historical working-capital levels.

The goal is to establish a **normalized level of working capital** and understand what is included in the purchase price.

## Inventory

For inventory-heavy businesses, inspect:

- inventory aging;
- inventory turns;
- obsolete items;
- shrinkage;
- valuation methodology;
- stock-outs;
- excess stock; and
- purchasing practices.

Book value does not necessarily equal economic value.

## Capital Expenditures

Separate:

- maintenance capex required to sustain the business; and
- growth capex used to expand it.

A business may report attractive EBITDA while requiring substantial recurring investment to maintain operations.

## Balance Sheet

Review:

- debt;
- accrued liabilities;
- taxes;
- leases;
- contingent liabilities;
- deferred revenue;
- customer deposits; and
- off-balance-sheet obligations.

The buyer needs a clear understanding of which assets and liabilities are being acquired.

# 6. People and Operational Due Diligence

A small business can be highly dependent on a few people.

## Owner Dependence

Map the owner's actual role.

Questions include:

- Which customers does the owner manage?
- Which suppliers?
- Does the owner sell?
- Does the owner price?
- Does the owner approve hiring?
- Does the owner manage cash?
- Does the owner hold technical knowledge?
- How many hours does the owner actually work?

Then ask what must happen after closing.

Possibilities include:

- existing employees absorb the work;
- the buyer assumes it;
- a new employee is hired; or
- the seller remains temporarily.

The cost of replacing the owner should be reflected in normalized economics.

## Key Employees

Identify:

- essential employees;
- flight risks;
- compensation gaps;
- retirement risks;
- knowledge concentration;
- tenure;
- incentive structures; and
- succession gaps.

For critical people, consider retention agreements or bonuses.

## Processes

Evaluate whether the company has:

- standard operating procedures;
- documented workflows;
- training materials;
- reliable systems;
- appropriate controls; and
- redundancy in key tasks.

The more knowledge that exists only in people's heads, the more transition risk exists.

# 7. Legal Due Diligence

Legal diligence is generally conducted by outside counsel.

Core areas include:

- corporate history;
- organizational documents;
- past acquisitions;
- litigation;
- background checks;
- intellectual-property ownership;
- employment matters;
- regulatory matters;
- leases;
- financing agreements;
- material customer contracts;
- material supplier contracts; and
- change-of-control provisions.

The commercial buyer should still own the business decisions.

A lawyer can explain legal risk and draft protection, but the buyer should not outsource judgment about whether a term is economically acceptable.

## Three Important Legal Concepts

### Representations and Warranties

Statements that specified facts are true.

### Covenants

Promises regarding future behavior, restrictions, or obligations.

### Indemnification

A mechanism through which one party may receive compensation for covered losses.

These are ways of allocating risk.

They do not eliminate the underlying business risk.

---

# 8. Capital Structure Is Part of Diligence

Due diligence should test not only the company but also the **proposed acquisition structure**.

A business that works with no debt may not work with acquisition leverage.

Questions include:

- What is debt-service coverage under the base case?
- What happens under a downside case?
- How much cash remains after debt service?
- Is there enough liquidity to absorb mistakes?
- Does seller financing create a balloon payment?
- Is refinancing required?
- How much working capital is available?
- What happens if growth is zero?
- What happens if EBITDA declines by 10%, 20%, or more?

The buyer should avoid a structure in which acceptable returns require nearly perfect execution.

---

# 9. Map Every Finding to an Action

A useful diligence tracker should not merely list issues.

For each item, record:

| Issue | Evidence | Risk | What Must Be True | Next Test | Response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Example: key supplier | 40%+ of gross profit depends on supplier | Red | Relationship continues after change of control | Supplier call + contract review | Walk / lower price / protection |
| Example: owner sales role | Owner handles top accounts | Yellow | Relationships transfer | Customer calls + transition plan | Retention / transition / hire |
| Example: inventory | Slow-moving SKUs | Yellow | Inventory value is recoverable | Aging analysis | Working-capital or price adjustment |

The final column is critical.

Possible responses include:

- proceed unchanged;
- lower the price;
- restructure the deal;
- require seller support;
- use an earn-out;
- use escrow;
- change working-capital terms;
- require retention arrangements;
- obtain legal protection; or
- walk away.

# 10. Common Diligence Errors

## Treating Diligence as a Checklist

An enormous request list does not necessarily produce insight.

Focus effort on the issues that matter to the thesis.

## Sunk-Cost Bias

Money already spent on accountants or lawyers should not affect the decision to continue.

A bad deal does not become better because diligence was expensive.

## Extrapolating History Blindly

Historical performance is evidence, not destiny.

Ask whether the factors that produced the history will continue.

## Believing Unsupported Projections

Future growth should be tied to observable drivers rather than optimism.

## Accepting Qualitative Claims Without Data

Statements such as:

- "customers are loyal";
- "the market is huge";
- "employees are strong";
- "demand is recurring"; or
- "there is plenty of pricing power"

should be converted into measurable questions.

## Ignoring Internal Inconsistency

If a company describes its current market narrowly but its growth market broadly, the investment story may be mixing incompatible definitions.

## Letting Advisors Own the Deal

Accountants and lawyers are essential specialists.

The buyer remains responsible for understanding the company and deciding whether to buy it.

# Worked Example: Spa Parts

The Spa Parts CIM provides a useful example of prioritizing diligence.

The company appears attractive because of:

- consistent historical earnings;
- fragmented customers;
- repeat demand;
- long vendor relationships;
- low capital intensity; and
- multiple operating improvement opportunities.

But the diligence plan should not begin with every conceivable request.

It should begin with the questions capable of changing the decision.

## Priority 1: Prove Normalized EBITDA

The CIM uses approximately $1.2 million of EBITDA in its valuation.

I would initially underwrite closer to **$1.0 million** until the adjustments are proven.

Key work:

- reconcile financials to tax returns;
- review bank activity;
- inspect add-backs;
- normalize owner compensation;
- separate COVID-era uplift;
- review monthly revenue and margins; and
- understand shipping-cost normalization.

If normalized earnings are materially lower, the effective purchase multiple immediately rises.

## Priority 2: Validate Balboa

Balboa is both a strategic asset and the biggest potential threat.

Before spending heavily elsewhere, I would want to understand:

- whether distributor status survives a change of control;
- pricing arrangements;
- termination rights;
- historical relationship;
- expected future relationship;
- Balboa's direct-to-customer strategy; and
- how much of Spa Parts' gross profit truly depends on Balboa.

If the relationship cannot be validated, this could be a walk-away issue.

## Priority 3: Inventory and Working Capital

With more than 6,000 SKUs, inventory requires significant attention.

Diligence should include:

- aging;
- turns;
- obsolescence;
- valuation;
- purchasing patterns;
- historical write-offs;
- stock-outs;
- vendor terms; and
- normalized working capital.

The purchase agreement should not force the buyer to pay full value for inventory that cannot realistically be sold.

## Priority 4: Employee Retention and Owner Replacement

The company has a small team and depends on technically capable inbound-sales staff.

I would determine:

- which employees are essential;
- which relationships they own;
- market compensation;
- expected retention;
- the owner's real responsibilities; and
- what replacement hires are required.

This affects both operating risk and normalized EBITDA.

## Priority 5: Stress-Test the Capital Structure

The downside case should examine the combined effect of:

- post-COVID demand normalization;
- lower normalized EBITDA;
- working-capital needs;
- employee replacement costs;
- supplier changes; and
- seller-note payments.

The point is not to ask whether each risk can be survived independently.

It is to ask whether the business can survive when several things go wrong at once.

# A Practical Diligence Sequence

For a typical small-business acquisition, my working sequence would be:

### Step 1 — Write the Thesis

Before requesting more information, state:

- why I want to own the business;
- what drives value;
- what the major risks are; and
- what must remain true.

### Step 2 — Identify the Five Questions That Could Kill the Deal

Do not start with a 200-item checklist.

Start with the five questions whose answers would change the go/no-go decision.

### Step 3 — Preliminary Diligence

Test those issues cheaply and quickly.

### Step 4 — Update the Model

Reflect what I learned in:

- normalized EBITDA;
- revenue assumptions;
- working capital;
- capex;
- debt capacity;
- valuation; and
- returns.

### Step 5 — Decide Whether to Continue

Explicitly decide whether the thesis still holds.

### Step 6 — Launch Confirmatory Diligence

Bring in accounting, legal, and specialist support once the major risks appear survivable.

### Step 7 — Maintain an Issue List

Track:

- finding;
- evidence;
- severity;
- owner;
- next step;
- financial impact; and
- proposed remedy.

### Step 8 — Re-underwrite Before Closing

Do not let the LOI valuation become an anchor.

Rebuild the investment case using what diligence actually proved.

---

# My Diligence Principles

1. **Start with the thesis, not the checklist.**
2. **Front-load the questions that could make me walk.**
3. **Understand the historical machine before forecasting improvements.**
4. **Ask what must remain true after closing.**
5. **Assume ownership transition itself can create risk.**
6. **Normalize earnings before thinking about the multiple.**
7. **Treat working capital and inventory as real uses of capital.**
8. **Stress-test the acquisition structure, not just the operating company.**
9. **Translate qualitative claims into evidence.**
10. **Use advisors for expertise, but retain ownership of the decision.**
11. **Map every material diligence finding to an explicit response.**
12. **Re-underwrite the deal before closing.**
13. **Do not let sunk costs turn a bad deal into a good one.**
14. **Expect every small business to have imperfections; determine which ones are survivable.**
