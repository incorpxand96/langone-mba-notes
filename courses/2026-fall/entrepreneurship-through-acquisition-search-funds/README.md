# Introduction to Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition & Search Funds

This folder is my working knowledge base for **MGMT-GB 2131: Introduction to Entrepreneurship Through Acquisition & Search Funds** at NYU Stern.

Rather than preserve the class as a sequence of lecture notes, I am organizing the material around the decisions an acquisition entrepreneur actually has to make:

> **What should I search for? → Is this a good business? → How do I engage the seller? → What should I pay? → How should I structure the deal? → What must I prove before I buy it?**

The notes combine course frameworks with my own application of them to cases, assignments, and potential future searches.

---

## Course Arc

The first four classes establish a useful progression:

1. **What is ETA?**  
   Understand the search-fund / acquisition-entrepreneurship model and what makes a searcher credible.

2. **What makes a business worth buying?**  
   Narrow the search, distinguish green / yellow / red flags, and evaluate business quality.

3. **Where and how do I find a business?**  
   Compare brokered and proprietary sourcing, engage brokers and sellers, and learn what must remain true after ownership changes.

4. **What happens once I find a deal?**  
   Negotiate price and structure, move from IOI to LOI, raise capital, and conduct business, financial, and legal diligence.

# The ETA Mental Model

When I encounter an acquisition opportunity, I want to be able to move through the following questions.

## 1. Is It a Good Business?

Look for:

- recurring or repeat revenue;
- low customer concentration;
- durable margins;
- strong cash flow;
- low capital requirements;
- defensibility;
- a capable and retainable team;
- a fragmented or attractive industry; and
- limited exposure to disruption or extreme cyclicality.

The class's typical ETA screening criteria include approximately **$1M–$4M of EBITDA**, at least **three profitable years**, and **greater than 15% EBITDA margins**, alongside qualitative business-quality criteria.

These are screening tools, not substitutes for judgment.

---

## 2. What Can Kill It?

Use a risk spectrum:

### Green

A genuine strength of the company.

### Yellow

A concern with a reasonable workaround.

Possible responses include:

- price adjustment;
- retention agreement;
- seller support;
- escrow;
- earn-out; or
- operating plan.

### Red

A potentially existential risk.

Examples might include:

- dependence on one unprotected customer;
- loss of a mission-critical supplier;
- financials that cannot be reconciled;
- a capital structure the company cannot support; or
- a business model that changes materially when the seller leaves.

The key is to assess the risk **after acquisition**, not just today.

---

## 3. Why Is the Owner Selling?

Seller motivation affects:

- likelihood of closing;
- transition;
- structure;
- negotiation;
- employee continuity; and
- the buyer-seller relationship after closing.

Questions include:

- Why sell now?
- What does the seller want from the transaction?
- How long will the seller remain?
- Does the seller care about employees or legacy?
- Is the seller truly prepared to sell?

A seller who is not psychologically or economically ready can waste enormous time.

---

## 4. How Was the Deal Sourced?

### Brokered

Advantages:

- seller is prepared for a process;
- financials may be cleaner;
- valuation expectations are established;
- the broker can educate the seller on structure.

Disadvantages:

- more competition;
- less direct seller access;
- time pressure;
- potential information filtering.

### Proprietary

Advantages:

- potentially less competition;
- more direct seller access;
- more time to learn the business;
- potentially better price or structure.

Disadvantages:

- slower;
- requires convincing the owner to sell;
- financials may require more cleanup;
- diligence can be harder.

Neither is inherently superior. The sourcing channel changes the transaction dynamics.

---

## 5. What Does Each Party Want?

A deal generally involves competing objectives among:

- seller;
- buyer;
- equity investors;
- lender;
- employees; and
- sometimes customers or critical suppliers.

The seller may care about more than price.

Possible priorities include:

- cash at close;
- legacy;
- employees;
- continued ownership;
- speed;
- certainty; and
- transition role.

Understanding those priorities creates negotiating room.

---

## 6. What Should I Pay?

Start with **normalized earnings**, not the multiple.

Then consider:

- quality and durability of cash flow;
- comparable valuations;
- concentration;
- growth;
- margins;
- capex;
- working capital;
- owner dependence;
- industry quality; and
- downside risk.

A good business is not necessarily a good deal at every price.

---

## 7. How Should I Structure It?

The five principal levers from class are:

1. acquisition debt;
2. seller note;
3. investor equity;
4. seller equity rollover; and
5. seller earn-out.

Structure affects:

- cash at close;
- ownership;
- incentives;
- risk allocation;
- debt service; and
- the company's post-close margin of safety.

The same headline purchase price can represent very different transactions.

---

## 8. What Must I Prove Before Closing?

The diligence framework:

1. **What am I buying?** — Understand the historical machine.
2. **What must remain true once I own it?**
3. **How does my buying it change the business?**

### Preliminary diligence

Ask:

> **What must be true, or I walk away?**

Front-load the issues that can kill the deal.

### Confirmatory diligence

Determine whether each remaining issue requires:

- no change;
- a price adjustment;
- an earn-out;
- seller support;
- contractual protection;
- another structural change; or
- walking away.

# My Current ETA Principles

These are the ideas I most want to carry beyond the class.

1. **Search narrowly enough to develop judgment.**
2. **Searcher-market fit matters: buy a business I am suited to operate.**
3. **Recurring revenue and durability usually matter more than exciting growth.**
4. **A CIM is a hypothesis, not evidence.**
5. **Translate every attractive claim into a diligence question.**
6. **Assess risk in the post-acquisition company, including leverage and seller departure.**
7. **Normalize EBITDA before debating valuation multiples.**
8. **Do not pay today for operational improvements I still have to execute tomorrow.**
9. **A moat can simultaneously be a concentration risk.**
10. **Price and structure are different questions.**
11. **Seller financing is debt; seller rollover is equity; an earn-out is contingent consideration.**
12. **Understand seller motivation before negotiating individual terms.**
13. **Winning an LOI is permission to diligence the deal, not a reason to fall in love with it.**
14. **Front-load the few diligence questions that can make me walk.**
15. **Expect imperfections in every small business; distinguish survivable problems from existential ones.**
16. **A great acquisition should work without heroic assumptions.**
17. **Leave a margin of safety for being wrong.**
18. **Know the price and terms at which I am willing to lose the deal.**

# Worked Case: Spa Parts

Spa Parts serves as a useful running example across these notes.

What initially makes the business attractive:

- long operating history;
- consistent historical earnings;
- fragmented customers;
- repeat purchasing;
- low capital intensity;
- long-standing vendor relationships; and
- multiple plausible improvement opportunities.

What requires deeper scrutiny:

- post-COVID normalization;
- more than 6,000 SKUs and inventory complexity;
- owner and key-employee dependence;
- unvalidated financial adjustments;
- substantial acquisition leverage; and
- especially the company's dependence on Balboa.

The case reinforces a central ETA principle:

> **The best thesis is not that I can fix the company. It is that I am buying a durable company at a sensible price and my improvements create additional upside.**
