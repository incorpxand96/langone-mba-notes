# Valuation in ETA

A good company is not necessarily a good acquisition.

In ETA, valuation sits at the intersection of:

- the quality of the business;
- normalized earnings;
- market expectations;
- deal structure;
- leverage;
- downside protection; and
- the buyer's required return.

The class exercise on Spa Parts framed valuation around three concrete inputs:

1. **TTM EBITDA of approximately $1.2 million** in the CIM;
2. **comparable multiples of roughly 3.5x–6.5x**; and
3. a **competitive bidding process**.

The buyer's job was not merely to choose a number in that range, but to **justify the multiple selected**.

> **Note on scope:** The specific Spa Parts facts and course framing above come directly from class. The broader framework below is my synthesized way of applying those ideas in future ETA deals rather than a verbatim valuation methodology from the course.

---

# 1. Start With Normalized Earnings, Not the Multiple

The most important valuation input is often not the multiple.

It is the denominator.

If a business is advertised at $1.2 million of EBITDA but sustainable post-close EBITDA is really $1.0 million, the effective purchase multiple is materially higher than the headline multiple suggests.

A basic relationship is:

> **Enterprise Value = Normalized EBITDA × Multiple**

That makes the definition of **normalized EBITDA** critical.

Questions include:

- Are seller add-backs credible?
- Does owner compensation need to be replaced?
- Was recent performance unusually strong or weak?
- Are there one-time revenue items?
- Are there expenses that will recur under new ownership?
- Is deferred maintenance missing from reported earnings?
- Will the buyer need new management hires?
- Are there post-close costs not present historically?

The guiding principle is:

> **Capitalize the earnings I believe will exist under my ownership, not the earnings the CIM asks me to believe.**

---

# 2. Headline Multiple vs. Effective Multiple

Consider a company offered at $5.2 million.

If EBITDA is truly $1.2 million:

\[
5.2 / 1.2 \approx 4.3x
\]

But if normalized EBITDA is actually $1.0 million:

\[
5.2 / 1.0 = 5.2x
\]

Nothing about the purchase price changed.

The economics did.

That is why quality of earnings and valuation cannot be separated.

---

# 3. The Multiple Should Reflect Business Quality

A multiple is shorthand for a much larger judgment about durability and risk.

Businesses deserve different multiples because they differ in:

- recurring or repeat revenue;
- customer concentration;
- margins;
- historical consistency;
- cyclicality;
- capital intensity;
- growth;
- competitive position;
- owner dependence;
- employee depth;
- supplier concentration;
- disruption risk; and
- ease of diligence.

All else equal, I would be willing to pay more for a business whose cash flows are more durable and less dependent on heroic assumptions.

Conversely, I should demand a lower price when I am taking material risk around:

- normalization of earnings;
- a key customer;
- a key supplier;
- owner transition;
- working capital;
- capex;
- cyclicality; or
- aggressive growth projections.

---

# 4. Valuation Is About the Business After I Buy It

The historical company is not exactly the company the buyer will own.

A transaction can introduce:

- acquisition debt;
- seller-note payments;
- additional management compensation;
- transaction expenses;
- lower cash balances;
- new working-capital needs; and
- transition risk.

Therefore I should not ask only:

> **What multiple does this business trade at?**

I should also ask:

> **What can this business safely support after the acquisition?**

A valuation that requires excessive leverage to finance may be too high even if comparable transactions appear to support the multiple.

---

# 5. Comparable Multiples Are an Input, Not an Answer

The Spa Parts exercise gave a wide comparable range of **3.5x–6.5x EBITDA**.

A range that wide demonstrates why "the comps say X" is not enough.

I still need to ask:

- Where should this particular company sit within the range?
- Are the comparables actually similar?
- Are they larger or smaller?
- Do they have more recurring revenue?
- Are margins better?
- Is customer concentration lower?
- Is management stronger?
- Is growth more durable?
- Was the comparable process more or less competitive?

A comparable multiple provides a market reference.

It does not remove the need for judgment.

---

# 6. Competitive Bidding Changes Price, Not Business Quality

The Spa Parts broker indicated that bidding was competitive.

That matters because the clearing price may rise.

But competition among buyers does not improve:

- EBITDA quality;
- supplier concentration;
- customer retention;
- management depth; or
- debt capacity.

This creates an important discipline:

> **Do not let auction pressure change the facts I am underwriting.**

A competitive process may require me to stretch.

It should not make me pretend risk has disappeared.

Sometimes the rational response to competition is to lose.

---

# 7. Separate Base Value From Upside

A seller may present many growth opportunities:

- pricing;
- outbound sales;
- digital marketing;
- geographic expansion;
- new channels;
- new products;
- acquisitions;
- cost reductions; or
- operational improvements.

Those opportunities may be real.

But I prefer to value a business primarily on what has already been demonstrated.

The strongest acquisition thesis is:

> **The current business justifies the purchase price, and my improvements create upside.**

The weaker thesis is:

> **The acquisition only works if I successfully execute several improvements after closing.**

The latter leaves much less margin for error.

---

# 8. Downside Cases Matter More Than Point Estimates

Valuation should be tested under scenarios.

At minimum:

### Base Case

What I genuinely believe is the most reasonable operating outcome.

### Downside Case

What happens if:

- revenue declines;
- margins compress;
- normalized EBITDA is lower;
- a key employee must be replaced;
- working capital consumes more cash; or
- growth is zero?

### Severe Downside

What happens when several risks occur at once?

The objective is not to predict the future precisely.

It is to learn whether the valuation and capital structure leave enough room to survive being wrong.

---

# 9. Price and Structure Must Be Evaluated Together

A $5 million acquisition funded with conservative leverage is not economically identical to a $5 million acquisition funded at the edge of what the company can support.

Likewise, the same headline price can contain different mixes of:

- cash at close;
- senior debt;
- seller financing;
- investor equity;
- seller rollover; and
- earn-out.

This means an acceptable valuation may become unacceptable under the wrong structure.

See [Deal Structure in ETA](deal-structure.md) and [Seller Financing](seller-financing.md).

---

# 10. Do Not Use Structure to Hide Overvaluation

Deal structure can legitimately allocate risk.

For example:

- an earn-out can bridge uncertainty about future performance;
- seller rollover can preserve alignment;
- a seller note can bridge a financing gap.

But structure should not be used to avoid admitting that the purchase price is too high.

A useful question is:

> **If I had to pay for this entirely with my own equity, would I still believe the business is worth this price?**

The answer does not determine the final structure, but it helps separate valuation judgment from financing availability.

---

# 11. Worked Example: Spa Parts

The class exercise used:

- TTM EBITDA of approximately **$1.2 million**;
- comparable multiples of **3.5x–6.5x**; and
- a competitive process.

The CIM valued the business at approximately **$5.2 million**, or about **4.3x the stated $1.2 million EBITDA**.

My own underwriting would be more conservative.

I would initially use approximately **$1.0 million of normalized EBITDA** until the financial adjustments are validated.

My comfort range would be approximately **4x–5x normalized EBITDA**, subject to diligence.

That implies:

| Multiple | EBITDA | Enterprise Value |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 4.0x | $1.0M | $4.0M |
| 4.5x | $1.0M | $4.5M |
| 5.0x | $1.0M | $5.0M |

I would move toward the upper end only if diligence provides confidence around:

- the Balboa relationship;
- inventory quality;
- employee retention;
- owner replacement;
- normalized earnings; and
- post-COVID demand.

At the stated $5.2 million price, the effective multiple on $1.0 million of normalized EBITDA is **5.2x**.

That may still be defensible.

But it is a different investment than a 4.3x deal on fully validated $1.2 million EBITDA.

---

# 12. What Would Make Me Pay More?

I would be more willing to pay toward the high end of a range for:

- truly recurring revenue;
- low customer concentration;
- strong customer retention;
- durable margins;
- a credible moat;
- low capex;
- low working-capital intensity;
- a management team that can operate without the seller;
- favorable industry structure; and
- multiple credible future buyers.

The common thread is **durability**.

---

# 13. What Would Make Me Pay Less?

I would push toward the low end, restructure, or walk away for:

- aggressive add-backs;
- customer concentration;
- supplier concentration;
- owner dependence;
- key-person risk;
- volatile margins;
- cyclical demand;
- heavy inventory;
- high capex;
- weak financial records;
- material transition costs;
- uncertain industry growth; or
- a deal requiring optimistic projections to service debt.

---

# 14. A Practical Valuation Process

For a future ETA target, my working process would be:

### Step 1 — Rebuild Earnings

Determine normalized EBITDA independently.

### Step 2 — Understand Quality

Evaluate recurrence, concentration, margins, capex, working capital, team, moat, and industry.

### Step 3 — Establish a Reasonable Range

Use relevant market references where available, but adjust for the company's specific strengths and risks.

### Step 4 — Build Downside Cases

Test lower revenue, lower margins, replacement hires, higher working capital, and other major risks.

### Step 5 — Overlay Deal Structure

Model debt, seller note, equity, rollover, earn-out, fees, and cash at close.

### Step 6 — Evaluate Returns and Resilience

Ask both:

- Is the return attractive?
- Can the company survive if I am wrong?

### Step 7 — Set a Walk-Away Price

Decide what I am willing to pay **before** auction dynamics and sunk costs take over.

### Step 8 — Re-underwrite After Diligence

The LOI valuation should not become sacred.

New evidence should change the price if the economics change.

---

# 15. My Valuation Principles

1. **Normalize EBITDA before debating the multiple.**
2. **A good company can still be a bad deal at the wrong price.**
3. **Comparable transactions are references, not answers.**
4. **Competition among buyers does not improve business quality.**
5. **Pay primarily for demonstrated earnings; treat execution upside as upside.**
6. **Evaluate the company after acquisition debt and transition costs are imposed.**
7. **Stress-test combinations of risks, not just the base case.**
8. **Price and structure must be evaluated together.**
9. **Do not use seller financing or earn-outs to disguise overvaluation.**
10. **Re-underwrite after diligence rather than anchoring to the LOI.**
11. **The best valuation leaves a margin of safety for mistakes.**
12. **Know the price at which I am willing to lose the deal.**
