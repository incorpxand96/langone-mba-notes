# Seller Financing

Seller financing means that the seller finances part of the acquisition price rather than receiving the entire amount in cash at closing.

In the ETA context, this usually takes the form of a **seller note**.

The seller becomes a lender to the buyer or acquisition entity and is repaid over time.

The simplest mental model is:

> **The seller is agreeing to wait for part of the purchase price.**

That can make a transaction easier to finance, but it does not make the deferred purchase price disappear.

A seller note is debt.

---

# 1. How a Seller Note Works

Suppose a business is being acquired for $6 million.

A simplified structure could be:

| Source | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Bank debt | $3.5M |
| Investor equity | $1.5M |
| Seller note | $1.0M |
| **Total** | **$6.0M** |

The seller might receive $5 million of value funded at closing and hold a $1 million note for the remainder.

That note may specify:

- principal amount;
- interest rate;
- repayment term;
- amortization schedule; and
- maturity or balloon payment.

In the course framework, seller financing is generally junior to senior acquisition debt.

---

# 2. Why Buyers Use Seller Financing

## Reduce Cash Required at Close

The most obvious benefit is that the buyer does not need to finance the entire purchase price with bank debt and investor equity on day one.

## Bridge a Financing Gap

A lender may be unwilling to fund enough debt, while the buyer may not want to raise enough equity to cover the remaining purchase price.

A seller note can fill part of that gap.

## Signal Seller Confidence

A seller willing to defer payment may be expressing confidence that the business will continue generating enough cash for the note to be repaid.

This is helpful information, but it is not the same as true equity alignment.

## Help Bridge Negotiation

Seller financing can help a buyer and seller agree on a headline valuation while changing the timing of payment.

That can matter when the seller cares about price but the buyer cannot or should not fund all of that value at closing.

---

# 3. Why Sellers Accept It

A seller note requires the seller to accept delayed payment and credit risk.

Possible motivations include:

- facilitating a transaction that otherwise would not close;
- achieving a desired headline purchase price;
- earning interest;
- demonstrating confidence in the business;
- meeting buyer or lender financing requirements; or
- accepting a trade-off between cash at close and total consideration.

The seller therefore needs to care not only about price, but also about the buyer's ability to operate the business and repay the note.

---

# 4. Seller Note vs. Seller Equity Rollover

This is one of the most important distinctions in deal structure.

## Seller Note

The seller is a **creditor**.

The seller expects:

- interest;
- repayment of principal; and
- priority based on the note's contractual position.

The seller generally does not receive incremental upside merely because the company becomes much more valuable.

## Seller Equity Rollover

The seller remains an **owner**.

The seller:

- shares in upside;
- shares in downside;
- remains economically exposed to the company; and
- may retain stronger incentives to support the transition and long-term performance.

The two instruments can reduce cash at close by the same amount while producing very different incentives and cash-flow consequences.

---

# 5. Seller Note vs. Earn-Out

These are also fundamentally different.

## Seller Note

Payment is generally owed according to the note terms.

It is **financing**.

## Earn-Out

Payment is contingent on future performance.

It is **contingent consideration**.

If the performance target is not achieved, some or all of the earn-out may never be paid.

This distinction matters when the buyer and seller disagree about future earnings.

If the seller believes EBITDA will grow dramatically but the buyer is uncertain, an earn-out may allocate that uncertainty more directly than a fixed seller note.

---

# 6. Seller Financing Does Not Eliminate Risk — It Moves It

From the buyer's perspective, a seller note can reduce the amount of cash required at closing.

But after closing it becomes a liability that must be serviced.

That means the buyer should model:

- annual interest;
- required principal payments;
- maturity;
- balloon payments;
- interaction with senior debt; and
- debt-service coverage.

A transaction can look financeable at close but become fragile later because the seller note consumes too much cash.

---

# 7. The Cash-Flow Problem

Imagine a company produces $1 million of annual EBITDA.

That EBITDA must potentially cover:

- taxes;
- working capital;
- capital expenditures;
- senior-debt payments;
- seller-note payments;
- management compensation; and
- reinvestment.

The headline EBITDA therefore overstates the cash available to the equity holder.

A seller note that makes the sources-and-uses table balance may still make the **post-close company economically unsafe**.

The correct question is:

> **Can the business comfortably service the seller note after accounting for everything else the company needs?**

---

# 8. Balloon Payments

A seller note may postpone principal repayment until a future date.

That can help near-term cash flow, but it creates a large future obligation.

For example, the Spa Parts transaction materials contemplated a seller note with a five-year term and a balloon payment.

That creates a specific question:

> **Where will the cash come from in year five?**

Possible answers might include:

- accumulated cash;
- refinancing;
- another financing transaction; or
- business sale proceeds.

If the structure depends on refinancing, then refinancing risk is part of the acquisition risk.

A future balloon should not be treated as somebody else's problem.

---

# 9. Seller Financing and Debt-Service Coverage

Seller financing must be considered alongside bank debt.

A buyer should model debt-service coverage under:

- base case;
- downside case; and
- severe downside case.

The important question is not simply whether the company can make the payment in a normal year.

It is whether there is still **a margin of safety** if:

- revenue declines;
- margins compress;
- a key employee must be replaced;
- working capital increases;
- inventory absorbs cash; or
- a supplier changes terms.

Fixed debt obligations and uncertain operating performance are a dangerous combination.

---

# 10. Seller Financing and Incentive Alignment

Seller financing can create some alignment because the seller still depends on the company's ability to repay.

But the alignment is weaker and different from seller equity.

A seller with a note primarily wants the company to remain solvent enough to make payments.

A seller with equity benefits from substantial long-term value creation.

This distinction became important in a class example involving an online-auction software company.

---

# 11. Worked Example: Seller Note vs. Seller Rollover

In the online-auction example, the contemplated structure originally included roughly **$2 million of seller equity rollover**.

The seller later countered by replacing the rollover with approximately **$2 million of seller financing**.

The headline dollars looked similar.

The economics did not.

## Original Rollover

The seller would:

- remain invested;
- participate in upside and downside;
- have incentives tied to the company's future success; and
- avoid creating the same fixed repayment burden.

## Seller-Note Counterproposal

The seller would:

- become a lender rather than continuing owner;
- expect repayment;
- add cash-flow pressure; and
- have less direct incentive to maximize the buyer's equity value.

The class takeaway was that the revised structure made cash flow substantially tighter and weakened the seller's alignment with the buyer.

This is a good example of why **structure cannot be evaluated by headline value alone**.

---

# 12. Worked Example: Spa Parts

The Spa Parts transaction contemplated seller financing alongside substantial bank debt.

The note carried a future balloon payment, which created particular concern in later years.

The issue was not that seller financing was inherently unattractive.

The issue was the interaction among:

- normalized earnings uncertainty;
- post-COVID demand uncertainty;
- senior acquisition debt;
- working-capital requirements;
- operating-transition risk; and
- the seller-note maturity.

If earnings turned out to be closer to $1.0 million than the higher advertised case, the fixed obligations would consume a greater portion of cash flow.

This reinforces a broader principle:

> **Evaluate seller financing using normalized earnings and downside cash flow, not just the seller's or broker's base case.**

---

# 13. When Seller Financing Is Especially Useful

Seller financing can be particularly attractive when:

- the business has stable cash flow;
- the seller strongly believes in the durability of the company;
- the buyer needs to reduce cash at close;
- the seller wants to achieve a particular headline valuation;
- the senior lender will not fund the entire acquisition;
- the note still leaves significant debt-service headroom; and
- the repayment schedule matches the company's cash-generation profile.

The key is that the note should support the acquisition, not merely make an otherwise overleveraged deal appear financeable.

---

# 14. When Seller Financing Should Make Me Nervous

I would be cautious when:

- the business already carries substantial acquisition debt;
- normalized EBITDA is uncertain;
- the seller note requires a large balloon payment;
- debt-service coverage is thin;
- the business is cyclical;
- working-capital requirements are volatile;
- capital expenditures are significant;
- the owner must be replaced at substantial cost; or
- the deal only works if aggressive growth projections occur.

In these cases, deferred payment can turn into deferred distress.

---

# 15. Questions to Ask About a Seller Note

Before agreeing to seller financing, I would want to understand:

## Economics

- What is the principal amount?
- What is the interest rate?
- When does interest begin?
- Is principal amortizing?
- Is there a balloon payment?
- What is the maturity date?

## Position in the Capital Structure

- Is the note subordinated to senior debt?
- How does it interact with lender requirements?
- Are payments restricted under certain circumstances?

## Cash Flow

- What are annual cash payments?
- What happens in the downside case?
- Can the company still invest in operations?
- What happens if working capital increases?

## Seller Incentives

- Is the seller also retaining equity?
- Is the seller staying during transition?
- Does the structure create useful alignment or merely a creditor relationship?

## Risk Allocation

- Would an earn-out better address uncertainty?
- Would rollover equity create better alignment?
- Should some of the purchase price simply be reduced rather than deferred?

---

# 16. Alternatives to Seller Financing

A seller note is one tool among several.

Depending on the problem, alternatives include:

- lower purchase price;
- more investor equity;
- more senior debt, if safely supported;
- seller equity rollover;
- earn-out;
- delayed or contingent consideration; or
- a different combination of instruments.

The financing tool should match the underlying issue.

For example:

- **uncertain future performance** may point toward an earn-out;
- **desire for seller alignment** may point toward rollover equity;
- **temporary funding gap** may point toward a seller note;
- **overvaluation** should probably point toward a lower price.

Debt should not be used to disguise a disagreement about value.

---

# 17. My Seller-Financing Principles

1. **A seller note is debt, not free money.**
2. **Reducing cash at close does not reduce purchase price unless the economics say otherwise.**
3. **Model seller-note payments together with senior debt.**
4. **Underwrite repayment using normalized earnings, not optimistic projections.**
5. **A seller note and seller rollover create very different incentives.**
6. **An earn-out is better suited to contingent future performance than a fixed note.**
7. **Watch balloon payments carefully.**
8. **Leave enough cash flow for working capital, capex, management, and mistakes.**
9. **A financing structure should preserve a margin of safety.**
10. **Do not use seller financing simply to make an overpriced deal close.**
11. **Ask what problem the seller note is actually solving.**
12. **The company's ability to survive the downside matters more than whether the sources-and-uses table balances.**
