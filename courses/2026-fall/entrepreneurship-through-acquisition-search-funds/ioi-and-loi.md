# IOIs and LOIs

The **Indication of Interest (IOI)** and **Letter of Intent (LOI)** mark two different stages in moving from interest in a business toward a transaction.

The course distinction is straightforward:

> **The IOI is marketing-oriented. The LOI is the detailed commercial framework that can become the basis for legal review and the eventual purchase agreement.**

Both documents communicate seriousness.

They do so at different levels of commitment and detail.

---

# 1. Context: You Are Still Selling Yourself

Before a buyer submits either document, the seller and broker are already evaluating the buyer.

The course emphasizes that the first interaction with a broker is already part of diligence **and** part of the buyer's own marketing.

A buyer should be able to explain:

- background;
- why the company is attractive;
- why the buyer is credible;
- how the acquisition can be financed; and
- why the buyer may be a good steward of the business.

The seller relationship matters because many ETA transactions require continued cooperation during transition.

The objective is not to impress the seller with complexity.

It is to **earn trust**.

---

# 2. Indication of Interest (IOI)

An IOI is a relatively light expression of serious interest.

The course frames it as a **marketing document**.

A good IOI should communicate:

- who I am;
- why I am the right buyer;
- strategic fit and value-add;
- an indicative price or price range;
- proposed structure;
- assumptions behind the offer, including EBITDA assumptions;
- how I expect to finance the transaction; and
- expected diligence process and timing.

The IOI is therefore doing two jobs:

1. communicating economics; and
2. persuading the seller that I am credible enough to keep in the process.

---

# 3. Plain English Wins

The course guidance on IOIs is explicit:

> **Do not overly lawyer it up.**

If the IOI requires substantial legal interpretation, it is probably too complex for its purpose.

It should be understandable to a business owner.

The IOI is closer to a thoughtful commercial proposal than a definitive legal document.

That means:

- clear language;
- transparent assumptions;
- simple structure;
- concise explanation of funding; and
- credible timing.

The buyer should not confuse sophistication with legal density.

---

# 4. Make Assumptions Explicit

An indicative price is only meaningful if the assumptions behind it are clear.

For example:

> "$4.5–$5.0 million enterprise value, assuming approximately $1.0 million of normalized EBITDA."

This is much more useful than simply stating a number.

Explicit assumptions help protect the buyer from appearing to retrade later if diligence reveals that the seller's stated earnings are not accurate.

Important assumptions might include:

- normalized EBITDA;
- debt-free / cash-free treatment;
- working capital;
- inventory;
- seller transition;
- customer retention; or
- continuation of a key supplier relationship.

Not every assumption belongs in every IOI, but the economic basis for the indication should be understandable.

---

# 5. Why Use an IOI?

The course raises the judgment question of when to use an IOI rather than moving straight to an LOI.

The useful role of an IOI is that it allows buyer and seller to determine whether there is enough alignment on:

- valuation;
- structure;
- buyer credibility; and
- process

before spending the time required to negotiate a detailed LOI.

It can therefore function as an intermediate filter.

> **Course note:** The deck explicitly raises "Why use IOI? Why not straight to LOI? When to skip?" as a discussion question rather than prescribing a universal rule. The choice is process-dependent.

---

# 6. Letter of Intent (LOI)

The LOI is substantially more detailed.

It establishes the commercial framework for the transaction and can become the basis for:

- legal review;
- due diligence;
- definitive documentation; and
- the purchase agreement.

The LOI should therefore resolve major business terms before the parties spend heavily on confirmatory diligence and lawyers.

---

# 7. What Goes Into an LOI?

The class materials identify several major areas.

## Buyer and Transaction

- who the buyer is;
- why the buyer is the right fit;
- specific plans to enhance the business;
- what is being acquired; and
- whether the transaction is structured as an asset or equity purchase.

## Purchase Price and Structure

- purchase price;
- debt;
- investor equity;
- seller note;
- seller equity rollover;
- earn-out;
- sources and uses; and
- cash at close.

## Seller Rollover and Governance

If the seller retains equity:

- cap table;
- employee option pool, if relevant;
- governance;
- board composition;
- deal fees; and
- ongoing fees.

## Working Capital and Escrow

- net working-capital methodology;
- escrow terms; and
- other closing adjustments.

## Seller Transition

- transition period;
- seller responsibilities;
- compensation; and
- continuing involvement.

## Legal and Process Terms

The deck lists items including:

- representations and warranties;
- covenants;
- operating restrictions;
- confidentiality;
- publicity;
- governing law;
- termination;
- closing conditions; and
- exclusivity.

The LOI is not the final purchase agreement, but it should be detailed enough that both sides understand the commercial deal they are pursuing.

---

# 8. IOI vs. LOI

| | IOI | LOI |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Primary purpose** | Express serious interest and market the buyer | Define the transaction framework |
| **Tone** | Commercial and persuasive | Commercial, detailed, increasingly legal |
| **Price** | Indicative range or proposal | More specific |
| **Structure** | High-level | Detailed |
| **Assumptions** | Key assumptions | Detailed economic and legal framework |
| **Funding** | Explain expected approach | Sources, uses, debt, equity, seller consideration |
| **Working capital** | Usually high-level if mentioned | Methodology should be addressed |
| **Seller transition** | Broad indication | Specific treatment |
| **Governance** | Usually limited | Important if rollover equity exists |
| **Exclusivity** | Not central | Often a key LOI term |
| **Relationship to definitive docs** | Early-stage filter | Basis for legal review and purchase agreement |

The progression is:

> **Interest → credibility → indicative economics → negotiated transaction framework → diligence → definitive agreement**

---

# 9. The LOI Is Where Structure Becomes Real

Before the LOI, it is easy to speak abstractly about price.

The LOI forces specificity.

For example, a "$6 million deal" could mean:

- $6 million cash at close;
- $4 million cash + $1 million seller note + $1 million rollover;
- $4.5 million cash + $1 million earn-out + $500K seller note; or
- many other combinations.

Those are not economically equivalent.

The LOI needs to make clear:

- what the seller receives;
- when the seller receives it;
- what is contingent;
- what is financed;
- and what ownership remains after closing.

See [Deal Structure in ETA](deal-structure.md).

---

# 10. Working Capital Belongs in the LOI

A buyer can agree on enterprise value and still have a major dispute later if working capital is undefined.

The LOI should address the methodology for determining net working capital.

This matters because the buyer expects to receive a business with enough working capital to operate normally after closing.

If the seller strips out too much:

- cash collections may lag;
- inventory may be insufficient;
- bills may still need to be paid; and
- the buyer may need to inject additional cash immediately.

Working-capital mechanics therefore affect the real economics of the transaction.

---

# 11. Seller Transition Should Be Negotiated Early

In many small businesses, the owner is not merely a shareholder.

The owner may manage:

- customers;
- vendors;
- employees;
- pricing;
- sales;
- technical knowledge; or
- daily operations.

The LOI should therefore address the seller's expected post-close role.

Questions include:

- How long will the seller remain?
- Full-time or part-time?
- What responsibilities?
- What compensation?
- What customer or supplier introductions are required?
- What knowledge needs to be transferred?

Leaving this vague can create major problems after signing.

---

# 12. Exclusivity Changes the Process

Once the seller grants exclusivity, the competitive process changes.

The buyer has won the opportunity to conduct diligence without the seller simultaneously negotiating with other bidders, subject to the LOI terms.

That makes the LOI a major milestone.

It also increases the danger of **sunk-cost bias**.

After winning the deal, the buyer should become more rigorous, not less.

The correct question remains:

> **What must be true, or I walk away?**

See [Acquisition Due Diligence](acquisition-due-diligence.md).

---

# 13. Use the "Reasonable Man" Standard

The course advises buyers to be:

> **Fair, kind, and reasonable.**

An LOI will eventually be reviewed by lawyers and inform definitive transaction documents.

That does not mean every possible term should become a battle.

A buyer should protect the economics and major risks of the deal while maintaining a workable relationship with the seller.

This is particularly important because the seller may still be needed after closing.

---

# 14. Brokers Are Agents, Not the Relationship

The course also cautions against allowing representatives to sink a deal.

Brokers can be valuable, but they may:

- communicate imperfectly;
- remain in the middle;
- misunderstand structure; or
- filter the parties' actual motivations.

Once under LOI, the class recommends ensuring direct communication with the seller, while keeping the broker appropriately involved.

The underlying principle is:

> **The buyer ultimately owns the commercial relationship and the transaction terms.**

---

# 15. From IOI to LOI: A Practical Sequence

My working sequence would be:

### Step 1 — Understand the Business

Review the CIM or available materials and identify key questions.

### Step 2 — Understand the Seller

Learn:

- why the seller is selling;
- what matters besides price;
- desired transition;
- concerns about employees;
- desired timing; and
- willingness to retain economic exposure.

### Step 3 — Establish Credibility

Explain:

- relevant background;
- financial backing;
- why the business fits;
- operating ambitions; and
- ability to close.

### Step 4 — Submit an IOI When Appropriate

State:

- indicative valuation;
- assumptions;
- high-level structure;
- funding approach; and
- timing.

### Step 5 — Learn From the Response

Determine:

- seller reaction to price;
- seller reaction to structure;
- competition;
- unresolved questions; and
- what matters most to the seller.

### Step 6 — Negotiate the LOI

Resolve major terms before expensive diligence begins.

### Step 7 — Win Exclusivity Without Falling in Love

Treat the LOI as permission to prove the thesis, not proof that the thesis is correct.

# 16. Common Mistakes

## Over-Lawyering the IOI

The IOI should communicate credibility, not show off legal sophistication.

## Being Vague About Assumptions

An offer based on "$1.2 million EBITDA" should say so.

## Focusing Only on Headline Price

Cash at close, seller financing, rollover, earn-outs, and transition matter.

## Leaving Working Capital Until Later

That can create a hidden economic renegotiation.

## Ignoring Seller Motivation

Knowing what the seller values can make negotiation more productive.

## Letting the Broker Own the Relationship

The broker facilitates; the buyer still needs direct understanding of the seller.

## Treating the LOI as the Finish Line

It is the start of serious diligence.

---

# 17. My IOI / LOI Principles

1. **The IOI is a marketing document: earn trust.**
2. **Use plain English and make the economic assumptions clear.**
3. **Explain why I am the right buyer, not just what I will pay.**
4. **Price and structure must both be communicated.**
5. **The LOI should settle the major commercial framework before expensive diligence.**
6. **Cash at close is not the same as enterprise value.**
7. **Address working capital before it becomes a closing dispute.**
8. **Define the seller's transition role early.**
9. **Understand seller motivations before negotiating hard over individual terms.**
10. **Protect important risks without turning every term into a battle.**
11. **Maintain direct understanding of the seller even when a broker is involved.**
12. **Winning the LOI means I have earned the right to diligence the business — not that I should buy it.**
