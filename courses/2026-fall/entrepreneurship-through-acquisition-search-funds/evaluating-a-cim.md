# Evaluating a CIM

A Confidential Information Memorandum (CIM) is a seller-side marketing document, not an objective description of a business.

The job of the buyer is therefore not just to understand **the story the seller is telling**, but to determine **what must be true for that story to hold up after acquisition**.

A useful CIM review should answer four questions:

1. **What is attractive about this business?**
2. **What could impair the business or the investment thesis?**
3. **Which concerns are fixable, and which could be existential?**
4. **What must I validate before spending significant time and money on diligence?**

The goal is not to eliminate all risk. Small businesses almost always have "hair." The goal is to distinguish manageable complexity from a flaw that can permanently impair the business or make the deal uneconomic.

---

## 1. Start With the Seller's Story

Before looking for flaws, summarize the investment case as the CIM presents it.

A strong first pass should capture:

- what the company sells;
- who its customers are;
- why those customers buy;
- how the company makes money;
- why revenue should recur;
- historical revenue and earnings;
- industry growth and competitive position;
- owner involvement;
- growth opportunities; and
- the proposed transaction and financing structure.

Then ask:

> **What assumptions does this story require me to believe?**

Those assumptions become the starting point for diligence.

For example, if the CIM argues that a business has a durable competitive advantage because of a key supplier relationship, the real diligence question is not whether the relationship has existed historically. It is whether the relationship will remain intact **after a change of control**.

---

## 2. Use a Green / Yellow / Red Framework

### Green Flags

Green flags are genuine strengths of the business: characteristics that increase the probability that the historical "machine" will keep working.

Typical examples include:

- recurring or repeat revenue;
- low customer concentration;
- strong and consistent cash flow;
- attractive margins;
- low capital intensity;
- a fragmented market;
- defensible customer or vendor relationships;
- long-tenured employees;
- low cyclicality;
- a simple, understandable business model; and
- credible paths to growth that do not require heroic assumptions.

A green flag should be something that can ultimately be verified, not just an adjective in the CIM.

### Yellow Flags

A yellow flag is a real concern with a plausible workaround.

Examples might include:

- owner dependence that can be addressed through transition support and hiring;
- overdue taxes that can be settled at close;
- weak sales processes that can be professionalized;
- underdeveloped systems;
- a repairable working-capital issue; or
- customer or employee concentration that can be mitigated through retention agreements.

Yellow flags often affect **price, deal structure, transition arrangements, or the post-close operating plan**.

### Red Flags

A red flag is a risk that could be existential to the company or the acquisition thesis.

Examples include:

- a major customer with no contract;
- a mission-critical supplier that can terminate the relationship;
- financial statements that cannot be reconciled;
- inadequate cash flow to service acquisition debt;
- fundamentally non-recurring revenue when recurrence is central to the thesis; or
- a business whose economics change materially when the owner leaves.

Importantly, risk should be assessed **post-acquisition**, not only based on the company as it exists today. Debt, ownership transition, employee departures, customer reactions, and change-of-control provisions can turn a tolerable pre-close risk into a major post-close problem.

---

## 3. Evaluate the Business Across Four Dimensions

### Financial

Questions to ask:

- Have revenue and EBITDA been consistent over several years?
- How much of earnings is recurring versus unusually strong in one period?
- Are management or broker adjustments credible?
- Do the income statement, tax returns, and cash flows reconcile?
- What is normalized EBITDA after replacing the owner's labor and removing questionable add-backs?
- What working capital is required to operate the business?
- How much cash is tied up in inventory?
- What maintenance capex is necessary?
- Can the business safely support acquisition debt?
- What happens to debt-service coverage in a downside scenario?

The most important principle is:

> **Underwrite the earnings you believe will exist after closing, not the earnings the CIM wants you to capitalize.**

A small change in normalized EBITDA can materially change the effective purchase multiple.

### Customer, Product, and Go-to-Market

Questions to ask:

- How concentrated are customers?
- Are relationships contractual or simply historical?
- Why do customers return?
- How easy is it to switch providers?
- Are products or services still relevant and in demand?
- Is growth driven by a repeatable process or by the owner?
- Are marketing claims backed by reliable data?
- Are there credible levers for growth or margin improvement?
- Is the business dependent on a single channel, platform, distributor, or partner?

A highly fragmented customer base is valuable, but fragmentation alone does not create a moat. The buyer still needs to understand retention, purchasing behavior, switching costs, and competition.

### Operations and Personnel

Questions to ask:

- Which employees are essential?
- Who might leave after a sale?
- Are key processes documented?
- Is there redundancy around critical functions?
- What does the owner actually do?
- Which roles must be replaced after closing?
- Is employee compensation competitive?
- Are incentives aligned?
- Does the company rely on specialized technical knowledge?
- Can the business survive the loss of one or two key people?

In a small company, a single employee can represent a meaningful percentage of organizational knowledge.

### Strategic

Questions to ask:

- Is the industry structurally attractive?
- Are the macro winds favorable?
- How cyclical is demand?
- How fragmented is the market?
- Who has leverage in the value chain?
- Are key vendor relationships documented and transferable?
- Could a supplier or partner move directly into the company's market?
- How vulnerable is the business to technological disruption?
- Are regulatory risks meaningful?
- What would be hardest for a new competitor to replicate?

---

## 4. Separate Historical Performance From the Post-Acquisition Business

One of the most useful ETA diligence questions is:

> **How does my buying the company change the company?**

Historical performance reflects a business with:

- its current owner;
- its current employees;
- its existing vendor relationships;
- its current capital structure; and
- no acquisition debt.

After closing, some or all of those conditions may change.

This means that a business that historically generated adequate cash flow can become fragile if:

- the seller leaves;
- key employees depart;
- a major customer or supplier reacts negatively to the sale;
- debt service absorbs most of the cash flow;
- working-capital needs increase; or
- the buyer immediately needs to hire additional management.

The relevant question is therefore not simply **"Is this a good business?"**

It is:

> **"Is this still a good business under my ownership and my capital structure?"**

# Worked Example: Spa Parts

## The Seller's Story

Spa Parts is a long-standing B2B distributor of hot-tub and spa replacement parts, with a smaller repair operation.

The CIM's case is attractive on its face:

- a long operating history;
- roughly $1 million of historical adjusted net income before the COVID surge;
- a highly fragmented customer base;
- recurring purchasing behavior from service technicians and e-commerce retailers;
- low seasonality;
- limited capital intensity;
- long-standing manufacturer relationships;
- one of only a small number of approved Balboa distributors at its pricing tier; and
- multiple apparent growth and margin-improvement opportunities.

The company also appears to have meaningful scale within a niche market: more than 6,000 SKUs, hundreds of active customers, and relationships with a large number of vendors.

That combination makes the deal worth investigating.

It does not make it automatically attractive at any price or structure.

---

## What I Like

### Consistent Earnings

The strongest part of the story is the historical earnings base.

The COVID period produced unusually strong demand, but the CIM indicates that Spa Parts generated roughly $1 million of adjusted net income even before the pandemic.

That creates a more credible starting point than a business whose profitability appeared only during an extraordinary period.

### Customer Fragmentation

The customer base is unusually fragmented.

No single customer appears large enough to threaten the entire company, which reduces one of the most common risks in a small B2B acquisition.

This is especially attractive because the customers appear to purchase repeatedly as they service and repair spas.

### Customer and Vendor Relationships

The company's long history matters because relationships appear to be an important part of its competitive position.

Customers rely on Spa Parts for access to a broad range of products, while the company has long-standing relationships with major manufacturers.

Those relationships may be harder for a new entrant to replicate than the physical distribution operation itself.

### Balboa Relationship as Both Moat and Risk

Spa Parts' position as one of a limited number of approved Balboa distributors is potentially one of its strongest strategic assets.

It may provide:

- access to important products;
- attractive economics;
- credibility with customers; and
- a barrier to new entrants.

But the same relationship creates the single largest risk in the deal.

That duality is important: **a moat can also be a concentration risk.**

---

## Yellow Flags

### 1. Post-COVID Demand Normalization

Demand increased significantly during COVID.

The problem is not simply that growth may slow. It is that the pandemic period makes it harder to determine the correct earnings baseline.

Questions:

- What did unit demand look like before, during, and after COVID?
- How much of the increase came from one-time purchases?
- Have repair volumes followed the installed base as expected?
- What do monthly results show after the pandemic surge?

**Mitigation:** Underwrite to normalized rather than peak performance and require sufficient debt-service headroom under a downside case.

### 2. Modest Pre-COVID Industry Growth

The CIM cites pre-COVID industry growth of roughly 2.2% CAGR.

That does not make the business unattractive, but it means the investment case should not depend on aggressive market growth.

**Mitigation:** Base the deal on durable existing cash flow and treat market expansion as upside rather than a requirement for acceptable returns.

### 3. More Than 6,000 SKUs

A broad product catalog helps customers, but it creates meaningful inventory complexity.

This is particularly important because inventory management is itself identified as an area needing improvement.

Questions:

- What are inventory turns by SKU?
- How much inventory is obsolete or slow-moving?
- How accurate is the inventory system?
- How much working capital is required to support the catalog?
- How often are write-downs taken?

**Mitigation:** Conduct an inventory-aging analysis, establish a normalized working-capital peg, and consider purchase-price adjustments for obsolete inventory.

### 4. Weak Vendor Economics

The CIM notes limited vendor terms and little history of negotiating discounts or rebates.

That may represent upside, but the opportunity should not be assumed.

**Mitigation:** Interview major suppliers during diligence and understand what economics are realistically available to a new owner before putting margin expansion in the base case.

### 5. Employee Retention

The company has only about a dozen employees and depends on technical inbound-sales personnel.

At this size, losing two people can be an organizational event rather than ordinary turnover.

**Mitigation:** Identify essential employees, benchmark compensation, use retention agreements where appropriate, document processes, and build redundancy around critical functions.

### 6. Owner Dependence

The owner appears involved in important operating and sales activities.

That raises the possibility that historical EBITDA understates the cost of replacing the owner's labor.

**Mitigation:** Map the owner's weekly responsibilities, determine what can be absorbed by the existing team, and include replacement compensation in normalized earnings.

### 7. Too Many Simultaneous "Opportunities"

The CIM presents many areas for improvement:

- pricing;
- inventory;
- outbound sales;
- online marketing;
- website improvements;
- vendor terms;
- direct-to-consumer sales;
- repair expansion; and
- incentive redesign.

That is potentially exciting, but it may also indicate an under-managed business requiring substantial operator attention.

For a first-time CEO, ten simultaneous initiatives are not necessarily ten sources of upside.

**Mitigation:** Prioritize a small number of low-risk, high-confidence improvements for year one and treat the rest as optionality.

### 8. Geographic Concentration

Customers are fragmented, but California represents a meaningful share of sales.

This is far less concerning than customer concentration, but it introduces exposure to local competitive, economic, or regulatory changes.

**Mitigation:** Review sales and profitability by geography and test whether the customer acquisition model can expand elsewhere.

### 9. Broker Adjustments Are Not Earnings

The CIM explicitly notes that financial diligence was still underway and that adjusted earnings rely on broker-provided adjustments.

That should prevent anchoring to the advertised EBITDA.

**Mitigation:** Reconcile reported results to tax returns, bank statements, and a Quality of Earnings analysis.

---

## Red Flags / Potential Deal Breakers

### 1. Balboa Supplier Concentration

Roughly half of parts are sourced from Balboa, and Balboa contributes disproportionately to profit.

The relationship is therefore not just an advantage. It is potentially existential.

Questions:

- Is the distributor status transferable after a change of control?
- Can Balboa alter pricing or terms?
- Can it terminate the relationship?
- Could Balboa sell directly to Spa Parts' customers?
- How important is Spa Parts to Balboa?
- What would EBITDA look like without Balboa?

**Mitigation:** Direct confirmation with Balboa should be a preliminary-diligence priority. If the relationship cannot be adequately validated, this could be a walk-away issue rather than merely a valuation adjustment.

### 2. Vendor Relationships May Not Be Contractually Protected

Long-standing relationships are valuable, but history is not the same thing as contractual protection.

A change in ownership may create a renegotiation opportunity for suppliers.

**Mitigation:** Review all material agreements, change-of-control provisions, historical correspondence, pricing arrangements, and termination rights. Obtain written confirmations where practical.

### 3. Leverage and Debt-Service Coverage

The proposed transaction uses substantial debt.

That is especially important because downside scenarios show limited debt-service headroom and the seller note includes a significant year-five cash requirement.

A business with good historical cash flow can still be a bad acquisition if the capital structure removes the margin of safety.

**Mitigation:** Lower the purchase price, increase equity, extend or amortize seller financing, or negotiate contingent consideration. The downside case should still leave enough cash to operate the company.

### 4. Post-COVID Normalization + Leverage

Demand normalization and high leverage interact.

Either risk might be manageable independently. Together they could produce a much worse outcome.

This is a useful general lesson: **risks should not be evaluated only one at a time.**

### 5. Normalized EBITDA May Be Lower Than Advertised

The transaction materials value the company using approximately $1.2 million of EBITDA.

I would be more comfortable underwriting closer to **$1.0 million of normalized EBITDA** until the adjustments are proven.

If normalized earnings are lower, the effective multiple rises immediately.

### 6. Seller-Note Balloon Payment

A large balloon payment creates refinancing or liquidity risk even if the company performs reasonably well in the preceding years.

**Mitigation:** Seek a longer amortization schedule, refinanceability protections, or a different seller-financing structure before closing.

---

## Valuation Approach

Comparable transactions may support a relatively wide multiple range, but a buyer should not select the multiple first and then justify it.

My current comfort range would be approximately **4x–5x normalized EBITDA**, assuming the key supplier relationship survives diligence and the historical earnings base is validated.

Using approximately **$1.0 million of normalized EBITDA** implies an enterprise value of roughly:

| Multiple | Implied Enterprise Value |
| ---: | ---: |
| 4.0x | $4.0M |
| 4.5x | $4.5M |
| 5.0x | $5.0M |

I would be reluctant to move toward the high end without strong evidence around Balboa, employee retention, inventory quality, and normalized earnings.

The central principle is that **price and structure are substitutes for certainty only up to a point**. A lower price can compensate for ordinary risk; it cannot fix an existential supplier problem.

---

## Preliminary Due Diligence Priorities

Before incurring major diligence expense, I would focus on the few questions capable of changing the decision.

### 1. Prove Normalized Earnings

- Reconcile financial statements to tax returns and bank activity.
- Review all add-backs.
- Normalize owner compensation.
- Examine revenue and margin by month, customer, SKU, and channel.
- Separate COVID-era performance from sustainable earnings.

### 2. Validate Balboa

This is the most important strategic diligence item.

I would want direct confirmation of:

- the relationship;
- distributor status;
- pricing tier;
- change-of-control implications;
- termination rights;
- expected future terms; and
- Balboa's broader channel strategy.

### 3. Understand Inventory and Working Capital

With more than 6,000 SKUs, the balance sheet matters almost as much as the income statement.

Key work:

- inventory aging;
- inventory turns;
- obsolete stock;
- stock-outs;
- purchasing practices;
- normalized working capital;
- vendor payment terms; and
- gross-margin performance by SKU.

### 4. Assess Employee Retention and Owner Replacement

- Identify key-person dependencies.
- Interview critical employees.
- Understand compensation and tenure.
- Determine who owns customer and vendor relationships.
- Build the cost of replacing the owner's role into the model.

### 5. Validate Industry Demand

The thesis should not depend on the CIM's optimistic post-COVID narrative.

I would want independent evidence on:

- installed hot-tub base;
- repair frequency;
- new-unit sales;
- replacement cycles;
- competitive intensity; and
- long-term parts demand.

---

## Mitigation Is Part of Valuation

A flag should not end with "this worries me."

The next question is:

> **What can I do about it?**

Possible mitigation tools include:

- lower purchase price;
- seller financing;
- seller equity rollover;
- earn-outs;
- escrow;
- working-capital adjustments;
- retention bonuses;
- transition agreements;
- customer or supplier confirmations;
- representations and warranties; and
- walking away.

The appropriate response depends on the type of risk.

A temporary earnings uncertainty might justify an earn-out.

A working-capital issue might justify a purchase-price adjustment.

A key employee risk might justify a retention agreement.

A supplier that can destroy the business may simply require a satisfactory answer before the deal proceeds.

---

## My Decision on Spa Parts

I would remain interested in Spa Parts, but **only at the right price and structure**.

The business has several characteristics I like:

- consistent historical earnings;
- highly fragmented customers;
- repeat demand;
- meaningful customer relationships;
- meaningful vendor relationships; and
- credible operational improvement opportunities.

But I would not underwrite the deal based on the CIM's optimistic case.

I would use approximately **$1.0 million of normalized EBITDA**, target a valuation around **4x–5x**, and make the Balboa relationship the highest-priority diligence question.

The strongest version of the thesis is not:

> "This company has many opportunities to improve."

It is:

> "This is already a durable cash-generating business, and I can improve it without requiring those improvements to justify the purchase price."

That distinction is central to ETA investing.

---

## General Takeaways

1. **A CIM is a hypothesis, not evidence.**
2. **Translate every selling point into a diligence question.**
3. **Judge red and yellow flags based on the post-acquisition business.**
4. **Normalize earnings before applying a multiple.**
5. **Stress-test combinations of risks, not just risks individually.**
6. **Growth initiatives should create upside, not rescue the underwriting.**
7. **Focus preliminary diligence on the few questions that could make you walk away.**
8. **Structure can mitigate risk, but it cannot cure a fundamentally bad business.**
9. **The best ETA acquisitions should work before heroic operational improvements.**
10. **Always ask: what must remain true after I buy the company?**
