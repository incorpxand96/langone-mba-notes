# Multi-Party Negotiation and Coalitions

Adding more parties changes negotiation qualitatively, not just quantitatively.

Two-party negotiation already contains:

- distributive elements;
- integrative elements;
- information problems;
- relationships.

Multi-party negotiation adds:

- more information-processing demand;
- decision rules;
- coalitions;
- vetoes;
- conformity pressure;
- constituencies;
- team dynamics; and
- hidden agendas.

---

# 1. Coalitions

A coalition is a subset of a larger group whose members agree to cooperate and divide the resulting benefit.

Coalitions matter because a party's power depends not only on individual resources but also on:

- who can combine;
- which combinations can control an outcome;
- which members are essential; and
- which members can be excluded.

---

# 2. Coalition Value

The Federated Science exercise demonstrates that coalition bargaining is not reducible to equal division.

Different pairs can generate different amounts of value.

A party with relatively little standalone value may still be powerful if it is pivotal to valuable coalitions.

The exercise also introduces formal approaches such as the **Shapley value** for thinking about marginal contribution across possible coalition orders.

The larger managerial lesson is simpler:

> **Power depends on contribution to feasible coalitions, not just on visible size or status.**

---

# 3. Stability

A coalition can be economically attractive and still unstable.

Members may defect if:

- another coalition pays more;
- they feel unfairly treated;
- they become pivotal elsewhere;
- interpersonal obligations are weak;
- the group includes unnecessary members.

A coalition must therefore satisfy both economics and relationships.

---

# 4. Harborco: Multi-Party Complexity

Harborco adds:

- formal veto power;
- multiple issues;
- several constituencies;
- secret voting;
- private caucuses;
- different reservation points.

The case makes clear that a deal can fail even when many individually acceptable agreements exist.

Why?

Because agreement requires the right combination of:

- substance;
- coalition support;
- decision rules;
- trust;
- process; and
- political feasibility.

---

# 5. Vetoes and Decision Rules

Before negotiating in a group, identify:

- who can block;
- what majority is required;
- whether votes are public or secret;
- whether excluded parties can re-enter;
- whether agreement requires consensus;
- whether particular parties have formal or informal vetoes.

Decision rules change bargaining power.

---

# 6. Constituencies

Representatives often negotiate on behalf of someone else:

- employees;
- voters;
- shareholders;
- regulators;
- departments;
- unions.

A forward-looking negotiator may become so focused on the room that they forget the people outside the room who must approve or live with the deal.

Always ask:

> **Who does each negotiator need to satisfy after leaving the table?**

---

# 7. Gains Strategies

The course distinguishes three broad approaches.

## Mutual Gain

- share and elicit interests;
- search for synergies;
- consider multiple alternatives;
- make trades;
- build relationships.

## Coalitional Gain

- find parties with aligned interests;
- construct alternatives together;
- recruit enough members to control the outcome;
- build obligations among coalition members.

## Individual Gain

- open aggressively;
- argue the merits;
- reveal interests selectively;
- appear difficult to move where useful;
- use deadlines or threats carefully.

A sophisticated negotiator can shift among the three.

---

# 8. Team Negotiation

When a party is represented by a team, organize roles.

Possible roles from the course include:

- task chief / manager;
- spokesperson;
- devil's advocate / tough role;
- relationship manager;
- data analyst.

Centralize information and authority enough to avoid contradictory commitments.

---

# 9. Preparation for Multi-Party Negotiation

Before the meeting:

1. Map all parties.
2. Identify formal authority.
3. Identify likely coalitions.
4. Identify personal relationships.
5. Identify allies and blockers.
6. Understand political and organizational context.
7. Set reservation points.
8. Choose a gains strategy.
9. Decide how the team will share information.
10. Model several feasible packages.

---

# My Rules of Thumb

- Map the decision rule before the issues.
- Identify veto players early.
- Model coalitions before entering the room.
- Do not recruit more coalition members than necessary without a reason.
- Remember constituencies.
- Watch conformity pressure.
- Hidden agendas are part of the negotiation.
- Silence can be useful.
- Mark concessions so they are remembered.
- Do not sacrifice the most important issue merely to preserve group momentum.
- In groups, process design is part of substance.
