# Biases: Anchoring and Framing

Negotiators do not process information neutrally.

The course uses classic judgment exercises to show how seemingly irrelevant reference points and equivalent descriptions can change decisions.

Two especially important biases are:

1. **Anchoring**
2. **Framing**

---

# 1. Anchoring

An anchor is an initial reference point that pulls later judgments toward it.

The important lesson is that anchors can influence even knowledgeable professionals.

The course examples include:

- multiplication estimates;
- salary estimates after exposure to low or high suggested salaries;
- fraud estimates by professional auditors;
- pricing menus.

The general pattern is:

> **The first salient number changes what feels plausible.**

---

## Negotiation Implications

### First Offers Matter

An opening offer can define the psychological range of discussion.

### Arbitrary Numbers Can Still Work

Knowing that an anchor is questionable does not necessarily eliminate its effect.

### Re-Anchoring Matters

If the other side anchors aggressively:

- avoid debating only within their frame;
- introduce a credible counter-anchor quickly;
- justify it with external reference points.

### Anchor Yourself Before the Meeting

A pre-defined aspiration and reservation price reduce the risk that the other side's number becomes my internal reference.

---

# 2. Reference Points

Reference points include more than explicit opening offers.

They can include:

- prior price;
- market price;
- budget;
- acquisition cost;
- appraisal;
- comparable transaction;
- list price;
- another bidder's offer;
- a "fair" return; or
- an apparently irrelevant decoy.

Negotiators should therefore prepare both:

- favorable references they can use; and
- responses to unfavorable references the other side may introduce.

---

# 3. Framing

Framing changes decisions by changing how the same underlying outcome is described.

The course examples contrast economically equivalent choices described as:

- gains vs. losses;
- jobs saved vs. jobs lost;
- money lost vs. tickets lost;
- paid ticket vs. free ticket.

People often become more risk-averse in gain frames and more risk-seeking in loss frames.

The underlying economics may be unchanged.

The psychological experience is not.

---

# 4. Ownership and Endowment

The painting example asks people to compare:

- the minimum amount they would accept to sell something they own; and
- what they would pay to acquire a similar item.

Ownership can create a higher subjective valuation.

This matters in negotiation because sellers can experience giving something up as a loss rather than as a neutral trade.

---

# 5. Sunk Costs and Mental Accounting

The theater-ticket and hockey examples expose how prior expenditures can affect current choices even when those expenditures cannot be recovered.

In negotiation, sunk-cost thinking can appear as:

- "We have already spent too much time to walk away."
- "We already paid the lawyers."
- "I have made too many concessions to stop now."
- "We cannot let the last three months have been for nothing."

The correct question is forward-looking:

> **Given the options available now, what outcome is best from here?**

---

# How I Think About It

Bias awareness does not make bias disappear.

The practical defense is structure:

- prepare reference points before the meeting;
- define reservation and aspiration levels explicitly;
- take time-outs before violating a bottom line;
- reframe choices in multiple ways;
- ask whether past costs are recoverable;
- separate ownership from economic value.

---

# My Rules of Thumb

- Assume I am anchorable.
- Write down my own numbers before hearing theirs.
- Introduce a counter-anchor, not just an objection.
- Reframe gains as losses and losses as gains before deciding.
- Ignore sunk costs in forward-looking decisions.
- Ask whether ownership is inflating my valuation.
- When a negotiation becomes emotionally "about winning," revisit the economics.
