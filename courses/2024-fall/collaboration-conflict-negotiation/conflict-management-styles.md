# Conflict Management Styles

The course uses five approaches to managing disagreement:

1. **Competing**
2. **Collaborating**
3. **Compromising**
4. **Avoiding**
5. **Accommodating**

The key idea is not to identify a single "best" style.

It is to develop the ability to choose a style that fits the situation.

---

# The Two Core Dimensions

The class framework places styles against two broad concerns:

- **importance of the substantive stakes**;
- **investment in the relationship**.

This creates a useful intuition:

- high stakes + lower relationship emphasis → more competing;
- high stakes + high relationship importance → more collaborating;
- moderate concern → compromising;
- low stakes + low relationship importance → avoiding;
- lower substantive stakes + high relationship importance → accommodating.

This is not a rigid formula.

It is a way to stop treating style as personality destiny.

---

# 1. Competing

Behaviors:

- assert opinions;
- state position clearly;
- stand ground;
- use rank or influence.

Potential strengths:

- protects interests;
- creates clarity;
- allows decisive action.

Potential weaknesses:

- reduces trust;
- hampers creativity;
- can minimize value creation.

---

# 2. Collaborating

Behaviors:

- ask questions;
- listen;
- consider joint needs;
- search for synergies;
- build trust.

Potential strengths:

- maximizes joint value;
- uses creativity;
- can improve relationships.

Potential weaknesses:

- consumes time;
- can weaken value claiming;
- can expose a cooperative party to exploitation.

Collaboration is powerful, but cooperation without boundaries is not strategy.

---

# 3. Compromising

Behaviors:

- seek middle ground;
- exchange concessions;
- keep the process relatively simple.

Potential strengths:

- resolves conflict quickly;
- gives each party part of what it wants;
- can equalize power.

Potential weaknesses:

- encourages inflated opening positions;
- can leave value on the table;
- may prevent deeper problem solving.

Compromise divides differences.

It does not necessarily create value.

---

# 4. Avoiding

Behaviors:

- delay engagement;
- reduce emotionally charged interaction;
- walk away where appropriate.

Potential strengths:

- lowers immediate tension;
- creates reflection time;
- can be strategically timed.

Potential weaknesses:

- delays resolution;
- frustrates parties ready to engage;
- can allow problems to escalate.

Avoidance is not always cowardice.

Sometimes it is timing.

---

# 5. Accommodating

Behaviors:

- yield to the other's needs;
- place relationship over substance;
- reduce time spent asserting personal interests.

Potential strengths:

- creates goodwill;
- signals cooperation;
- can build relationships strategically.

Potential weaknesses:

- reduces influence;
- may be perceived as weakness;
- can eliminate joint problem solving.

Accommodation should be deliberate, not automatic.

---

# Three Types of Goals

The course asks negotiators to consider:

### Relational Goals

What relationship do I want after this conflict?

### Substantive Goals

How important are the stakes and what is the nature of the issue?

### Temporal Goals

Is this:

- one-shot or repeated?
- short term or long term?
- urgent or deferrable?

Style should follow goals.

---

# How I Think About It

The useful self-awareness question is not:

> **What is my style?**

It is:

> **Which styles do I default to, and which styles do I fail to use when the situation calls for them?**

A style profile is therefore developmental rather than diagnostic.

---

# My Rules of Thumb

- Match style to stakes and relationship.
- Do not collaborate reflexively with an exploitative counterparty.
- Do not compete reflexively when joint value exists.
- Use compromise for speed, not because halfway is inherently fair.
- Use avoidance intentionally.
- Accommodate when the relationship value exceeds the substantive concession.
- Build range rather than optimizing one favorite style.
