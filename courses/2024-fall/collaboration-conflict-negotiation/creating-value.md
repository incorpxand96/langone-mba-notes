# Creating Value

A negotiation can involve two distinct tasks:

1. **Creating value** — enlarging the total surplus.
2. **Claiming value** — deciding how the surplus is divided.

Distributive bargaining focuses primarily on the second.

Integrative bargaining asks whether the parties can make the pie larger before fighting over slices.

---

# 1. Do Not Assume a Fixed Pie

The central integrative insight is:

> **The other party is unlikely to value every issue exactly the way I do.**

Differences can arise from:

- priorities;
- costs;
- capabilities;
- time preferences;
- risk preferences;
- forecasts;
- constraints; and
- tastes.

Those differences create opportunities for trade.

---

# 2. Interests vs. Positions

A **position** is what someone says they want.

An **interest** is why they want it.

Example:

Position:

> "I need a higher price."

Possible interests:

- cash today;
- certainty;
- status;
- fairness;
- financing needs;
- risk reduction;
- internal approval.

Once interests are visible, additional ways to solve the problem may appear.

---

# 3. Trading on Differences

The Working Moms exercise shows several mechanisms.

## Different Priorities

One party may care far more about one issue than the other.

Give on low-value issues to receive value on high-value issues.

## Different Time Preferences

The parties may value money at different times differently.

Payment timing can therefore create or destroy surplus.

## Different Forecasts

If parties genuinely disagree about the future, they do not need to keep arguing.

They can use a **contingent contract** that pays differently depending on what happens.

## Different Risk Preferences

Risk can sometimes be reallocated toward the party better able or more willing to bear it.

---

# 4. Multi-Issue Packages

Do not negotiate every issue sequentially.

Single-issue bargaining hides trade-offs.

Instead:

- discuss multiple issues;
- make package proposals;
- use "what if?" questions;
- compare alternatives;
- withhold final approval until the package is understood.

A package reveals how the other side values combinations.

---

# 5. Information Strategy

Integrative bargaining requires information.

But information sharing is not all-or-nothing.

The course suggests:

- build trust;
- ask questions;
- listen;
- indicate what is important and where I am flexible;
- give away some information;
- insist on reciprocity;
- use trial balloons;
- make multiple offers simultaneously.

The goal is to reveal enough about **relative priorities** to discover trades without casually disclosing a reservation price.

---

# 6. Contingent Contracts

Contingent contracts are especially useful when parties disagree about uncertain future outcomes.

Potential benefits include:

- turning differences into value;
- testing sincerity;
- sharing risk;
- improving incentives;
- managing bias;
- solving some trust problems.

The basic idea:

> **Do not keep arguing about which forecast is correct. Design a deal that pays differently depending on which forecast proves correct.**

---

# 7. Post-Settlement Settlements

Reaching agreement does not mean the current agreement is efficient.

After a tentative deal, parties can ask:

> **Can we find another package that makes at least one party better off without making the other worse off?**

This is a useful discipline because the psychological pressure to "be done" can stop value creation too early.

---

# Worked Example: Working Moms

The case has three major issues:

- price per title;
- number of runs;
- payment timing.

The parties value additional runs differently.

At certain levels, extra runs help one side more than they hurt the other.

That creates surplus.

Similarly, they value payment timing differently.

The exercise demonstrates that the best deal is not necessarily a compromise on each issue.

It is a package that exploits differences in valuations.

---

# Creating vs. Claiming

Integrative negotiation does not mean abandoning self-interest.

After creating value, the parties still need to divide it.

Collaboration can therefore increase the pie while distributive skill determines how much of the larger pie I receive.

The best negotiator needs both.

---

# My Rules of Thumb

- Assume multiple issues contain hidden trades.
- Ask which issue matters most and least to each side.
- Trade, do not merely compromise.
- Use packages.
- Use trial balloons.
- Share information about priorities more readily than about bottom lines.
- Require reciprocity.
- Use contingent contracts for genuine forecast disagreement.
- Keep searching after a tentative agreement.
- Create value before fighting exclusively about its division.
