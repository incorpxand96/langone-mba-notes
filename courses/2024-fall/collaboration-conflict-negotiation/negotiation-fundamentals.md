# Negotiation Fundamentals

The course begins with a small set of concepts that should be explicit before almost any negotiation:

1. **BATNA**
2. **Reservation price**
3. **Bargaining zone / ZOPA**
4. **Aspiration level**
5. **Reference points**
6. **Preparation**

These concepts are most visible in distributive bargaining, but they remain useful in integrative and multi-party negotiations.

---

# 1. BATNA

**BATNA** = **Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement**.

It answers:

> **What will I actually do if I do not reach agreement here?**

BATNA is a major source of negotiating power because it determines how costly impasse is.

A strong BATNA gives freedom to reject a weak deal.

A weak BATNA makes agreement more valuable — but does not mean I should reveal that weakness.

## Practical Rules

- Identify the specific alternative, not a vague sense of "other options."
- Estimate its value.
- Strengthen the BATNA before negotiating when possible.
- Keep options open.
- Reveal a strong BATNA selectively.
- Do not bluff about a BATNA I do not have.

---

# 2. Reservation Price

The **reservation price** is the least favorable outcome I am willing to accept before preferring impasse.

It is derived from the BATNA, adjusted for deal-specific considerations such as:

- switching costs;
- timing;
- opportunity cost;
- non-financial preferences;
- relationship value; and
- execution risk.

The course's key discipline is to define the reservation price **before** bargaining.

Otherwise, emotion, anchoring, sunk costs, or pressure can move the line during the negotiation.

## Do Not Reveal It

A reservation price is different from an opening offer.

If the other party learns my true bottom line, that number can become their target.

---

# 3. Bargaining Zone / ZOPA

The **Zone of Possible Agreement (ZOPA)** exists when the buyer's reservation price is above the seller's reservation price.

Example:

- Seller will accept at least $17M.
- Buyer will pay up to $25M.

The bargaining zone is $17M–$25M.

If the buyer's maximum is below the seller's minimum, there is a **negative bargaining zone**.

That does not always mean the negotiation is hopeless.

It may mean:

- another issue needs to be added;
- an underlying interest needs to be uncovered;
- timing or risk can be restructured;
- a non-monetary term can create value; or
- no mutually beneficial agreement actually exists.

---

# 4. Aspiration Level

A reservation price is a floor or ceiling.

It should not become the target.

An **aspiration level** is an ambitious but defensible outcome I am trying to achieve.

Without an aspiration, negotiators can become anchored on merely reaching agreement.

The course encourages negotiators to **anchor themselves upward** by explicitly defining a reasonable aspiration before the conversation begins.

---

# 5. First Offers and Anchoring

First offers matter because they establish a reference point.

Course guidance:

- learn the market;
- make an ambitious but credible first offer when sufficiently informed;
- allow the other party to open when I truly lack information;
- if they make an unfavorable first offer, **re-anchor quickly**.

The logic is not that the first offer determines the final deal.

It changes the range that feels psychologically salient.

---

# 6. Objective Reference Points

Useful references may include:

- market price;
- list price;
- comparable transactions;
- prior price;
- budget;
- other bids;
- industry standards;
- fair-market data; and
- reasonable return expectations.

Prepare multiple reference points before negotiating.

The other side will likely have its own.

The task is to understand which reference points are economically relevant and which merely benefit the party proposing them.

---

# 7. Preparation

One of the strongest course themes is that negotiation success depends heavily on work done away from the table.

A preparation document should answer:

### My Side

- What are my issues?
- What are my interests?
- What is my BATNA?
- What is my reservation price?
- What is my aspiration?
- What reference points support my case?
- What is my preferred style?
- What do I care about long term?

### Their Side

- What is their likely BATNA?
- What are their interests?
- What are their likely reservation and aspiration points?
- What pressures do they face?
- What information do they have that I do not?
- What relationship do they want after the negotiation?

### Process

- Who should make the first offer?
- What information should I seek first?
- What should I disclose?
- What concession pattern do I want?
- When should I pause?
- What would make me walk away?

# Worked Example: Dosagen–Synertech

Dosagen wants to sell a biotech plant.

Its alternative is to dismantle and sell the assets separately for about **$17M**.

Synertech's alternative is to build a new plant for about **$25M**, with additional timing and approval costs.

At the simplest level, those alternatives create a positive bargaining zone.

But the exercise also demonstrates that negotiators can support very different opening positions with apparently objective data:

- historical purchase price;
- appraisal value;
- comparable sale;
- construction cost; and
- broader real-estate conditions.

The lesson is that "objective criteria" are not self-interpreting.

Preparation means knowing which facts matter and how the other side will frame them.

---

# My Rules of Thumb

- BATNA first.
- Reservation price second.
- Aspiration third.
- Never confuse the bottom line with the target.
- Keep the likely ZOPA in mind.
- Do not reveal my reservation price.
- Strengthen alternatives before asking for concessions.
- Prepare several defensible reference points.
- Re-anchor when necessary.
- If there is no ZOPA, search for another dimension before assuming compromise can solve it.
