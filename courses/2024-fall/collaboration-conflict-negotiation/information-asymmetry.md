# Information Asymmetry

Negotiators rarely know the same things.

Information asymmetry can create value when differences are traded intelligently.

It can also create adverse selection when the better-informed party controls whether an offer is accepted.

The **Acquiring a Company** exercise illustrates the second problem.

---

# The Setup

Company B's true standalone value is somewhere from $0 to $100 per share.

B knows the result.

The buyer does not.

Under the buyer's management, the company would be worth **50% more** than its standalone value.

At first glance, the acquisition appears attractive because the buyer can create value.

But acceptance itself reveals information.

---

# The Selection Problem

Suppose the buyer offers **Y**.

B accepts only when its true value is at or below Y.

Conditional on acceptance, the expected standalone value is therefore approximately:

> **Y / 2**

Because the buyer can create 50% more value, the expected value to the buyer becomes:

> **1.5 × (Y/2) = 0.75Y**

But the buyer pays Y.

Expected value conditional on acceptance:

> **0.75Y - Y = -0.25Y**

Any positive offer creates an expected loss under the exercise's assumptions.

The apparently valuable synergy does not rescue the buyer because the seller knows when the buyer is overpaying.

---

# The Lesson

Do not evaluate an offer using the unconditional expected value of the asset.

Ask:

> **What does acceptance tell me?**

This is a powerful general rule for:

- acquisitions;
- hiring;
- insurance;
- lending;
- procurement;
- used assets;
- marketplaces.

The counterparty's willingness to transact can itself contain information.

---

# Strategic Reasoning

The course pairs this with a "beauty contest" game in which the optimal action depends on reasoning about how other participants reason.

The broader point is that negotiation is not just:

> What do I want?

It is also:

> What do they know, what do they think I know, and how will they respond to what they think I will do?

---

# Information Strategy

The course's final reminders are deliberately balanced:

- gather information aggressively;
- listen;
- understand the other side's perspective;
- recognize that information may be asymmetric;
- be selective about what I disclose.

The challenge is not "share" versus "hide."

It is identifying which information:

- creates joint value when shared;
- protects leverage when withheld; and
- changes interpretation when revealed.

---

# My Rules of Thumb

- Treat acceptance as information.
- Ask what selection mechanism produced the counterparty in front of me.
- Distinguish unconditional value from value conditional on the other side's behavior.
- Share relative priorities more freely than reservation prices.
- Be especially careful when the other side knows asset quality better than I do.
- Design contingent terms when uncertainty can be observed later.
- Think at least one move beyond the immediate offer.
