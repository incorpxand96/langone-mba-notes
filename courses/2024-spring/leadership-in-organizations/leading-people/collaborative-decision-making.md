# Collaborative Decision-Making

Groups have greater potential than the average individual.

But group potential is often unrealized.

A group can make worse decisions than its members would have made independently.

The leader therefore has to design the **decision process**, not just debate content.

---

# Individual Judgment Biases

## Overconfidence

Subjective confidence exceeds objective accuracy.

Examples:

- forecasting;
- project estimates;
- entrepreneurship.

## Availability

Information that comes to mind easily is treated as more likely or important.

Memory is not a neutral database.

## Framing / Prospect Theory

People evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point.

Typical pattern:

- risk-averse when gains are emphasized;
- risk-seeking when losses are emphasized.

Losses loom larger than equivalent gains.

## Sunk Cost / Escalation of Commitment

People continue investing because of prior investment rather than future value.

Remedies:

- treat sunk costs as sunk;
- focus on future returns;
- define exit rules in advance.

## Anchoring and Adjustment

Initial numbers or salient information influence later judgment even when the anchor is irrelevant.

---

# Group Benefits

Groups can outperform individuals when they successfully combine:

- information;
- expertise;
- perspectives;
- error correction.

The challenge is accessing the information distributed across members.

---

# Group Pitfalls

## Groupthink

Pressure toward consensus suppresses dissent.

Drivers include:

- desire to fit in;
- conformity pressure;
- discomfort with disagreement;
- overconfidence in the group;
- closed-mindedness;
- time pressure.

## Abilene Paradox

A group collectively chooses an option that none of the individuals actually prefers.

One cause is **pluralistic ignorance**:

> each person assumes others support something that they do not.

## Group Polarization

Discussion pushes average group judgment toward a more extreme position.

Causes can include:

- common knowledge effects;
- conformity;
- normative influence;
- status competition.

---

# Improve Group Decisions

Before deciding:

- clarify process;
- manage team size;
- solicit different perspectives;
- use a devil's advocate;
- ask "what if we did the opposite?";
- record alternatives;
- require a second solution;
- reduce avoidable time pressure.

Also ask:

> Is my own preferred solution self-serving?

---

# My Take

Good group decision-making is not just "get smart people in a room."

It requires architecture.
