# Motivation and Incentives

Motivation affects:

- direction of behavior;
- effort;
- persistence.

Skills and experience create little value if the system produces weak motivation.

---

# Sources of Motivation

## Extrinsic

Examples:

- pay;
- bonus;
- promotion;
- status;
- punishment.

## Intrinsic / Psychological

Examples:

- challenge;
- learning;
- meaningfulness;
- growth;
- fairness;
- autonomy;
- social connection.

People differ in what they value.

---

# Needs

Needs theories emphasize that people care about more than money.

Relevant needs include:

- security/existence;
- belonging/relatedness;
- esteem;
- growth;
- self-actualization.

---

# Two-Factor Logic

## Hygiene Factors

Their absence creates dissatisfaction.

Examples:

- pay/security;
- working conditions;
- company policies;
- supervision;
- relationships.

Their presence does not necessarily create deep motivation.

## Motivators

Examples:

- achievement;
- recognition;
- responsibility;
- work itself;
- growth.

These can increase satisfaction and motivation.

---

# Equity and Fairness

People compare:

> inputs contributed / outcomes received

with relevant others and past precedent.

Perceived inequity can reduce motivation even when absolute compensation is high.

Fairness is therefore a powerful motivator.

---

# Expectancy Theory

Motivation depends on:

- **Expectancy:** effort → performance
- **Instrumentality:** performance → outcome
- **Valence:** value of the outcome

If any link is weak, the incentive loses motivational power.

---

# Goal Setting

Goals tend to work better when they are:

- specific;
- measurable where appropriate;
- challenging;
- realistic;
- accepted.

Goals help by:

- increasing effort;
- directing attention;
- encouraging strategy;
- increasing persistence.

---

# Incentives as Control Systems

Incentives attempt to align employee and organizational goals.

But they also communicate:

> **what the organization really values.**

This creates the classic risk:

> **rewarding A while hoping for B.**

---

# Downsides of Pay for Performance

Potential problems:

- poor measurement;
- reduced teamwork;
- short-termism;
- gaming;
- unethical behavior;
- reduced learning;
- crowding out intrinsic motivation.

Pay-for-performance works best when:

- performance is objective;
- measurement is easy;
- coordination needs are low;
- tasks are routine;
- intrinsic interest is less central.

---

# Job Design

Intrinsic motivation can be strengthened through:

- skill variety;
- task identity;
- task significance;
- autonomy;
- feedback.

These can produce:

- meaningfulness;
- responsibility;
- knowledge of results.

---

# My Take

The question is not:

> "How much should we pay?"

It is:

> **"What behavior and psychological experience does this system create?"**
