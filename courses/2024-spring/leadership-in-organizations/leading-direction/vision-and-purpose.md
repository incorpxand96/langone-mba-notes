# Vision and Purpose

The course treats vision as a tool for puncturing the "gravity" of status quo thinking.

A vision describes a future state that can guide behavior.

But abstract aspirations often fail to motivate.

---

# Paradox of Purpose

Ultimate aspirations tend to be:

- timeless;
- abstract;
- far-reaching.

Daily work is:

- time-constrained;
- concrete;
- narrow.

The leader has to bridge the two.

---

# Effective Visions

Effective visions:

1. **Engage emotion with a vivid, image-laden future.**
2. **Link the future to daily work.**
3. **Create a shared reference point for collective action.**
4. **Help leaders visualize what success actually looks like.**

---

# Concrete vs. Specific

Specific does not always mean concrete.

Example:

> "30% more"

is numerically specific but hard to visualize.

Visions often benefit from:

- imagery;
- people;
- behavior;
- scenes;
- concrete outcomes.

Goals can then carry the numeric specificity.

---

# Vision → Goals

Vision answers:

> What future are we trying to create?

Goals answer:

> What must happen, and by when, to make that future real?

Goals should follow from vision.

---

# Time Machine Test

Imagine being transported several years forward after the vision has succeeded.

Ask:

- What is different?
- What do people see?
- What do they say?
- What behavior has changed?
- What does success look like?

Translate that mental movie into language.

---

# Personal Example: Everything Is Noise

My in-class vision evolved from a generic market-position statement toward a more customer-centered idea:

> become the premier destination for dedicated fans to read more about bands they already love and discover new bands they will love.

The exercise made the emotional end state more concrete than a generic claim about being "number one."

---

# My Take

Metrics make strong goals.

Images make strong visions.

A leader needs both.
