# Mount Everest — 1996

## Core Question

How did experienced climbers and guides end up in a catastrophic outcome despite extensive preparation and expertise?

The course's strongest answer is:

> **Do not look for one cause.**

---

# Individual Factors

- overconfidence / false hope;
- escalation of commitment;
- fatigue;
- abandoned turnaround plans;
- weak contingency planning;
- summit fever.

Doug Hansen's prior near-miss, for example, created a powerful psychological commitment to reaching the summit.

---

# Group Factors

- hierarchy;
- no speaking up;
- low trust;
- weak cohesion;
- poor communication;
- limited radio access;
- inadequate delegation.

Strong leader authority can be useful in dangerous environments.

It can also suppress information from below.

---

# System Factors

- weather;
- oxygen;
- logistics;
- route preparation;
- bottlenecks;
- tight interconnectedness;
- commercial-expedition structure.

Small failures accumulated and interacted.

---

# Decision Architecture

The expedition had planned constraints:

- oxygen limits;
- turnaround assumptions;
- guide roles.

But execution drifted.

One important lesson is that a rule is only useful if the system makes adherence likely.

---

# Leadership Lessons

- create explicit exit rules before emotion escalates;
- distinguish confidence from calibration;
- build communication redundancy;
- create speaking-up mechanisms;
- do not over-centralize all judgment;
- plan for leader fatigue;
- use pre-mortems and contingencies;
- understand coupling among failures.

---

# My Take

Everest is not primarily a story about incompetence.

It is a story about how competent people can become trapped by:

- bias;
- hierarchy;
- goal fixation;
- communication breakdown;
- system interaction.

That makes it much more useful.
