# International Trade

## Comparative Advantage

Trade can benefit both countries even when one has an absolute productivity advantage in everything.

What matters is comparative advantage: relative opportunity cost.

Trade expands aggregate consumption possibilities.

## Positive-Sum, Not Distribution-Neutral

The course's core conclusion:
- aggregate gains from trade are positive;
- not everyone gains.

Trade can lower prices and reallocate production while imposing concentrated losses on some workers and industries.

## Trade Balance

`NX = X − M`

The textbook links external balance to saving and investment:

`S = I + NX`

A trade deficit can reflect low saving, high investment, or both. Whether borrowing is concerning depends partly on what it finances.

## Foreign Direct Investment

FDI involves ownership/control of a foreign business interest.

- **Outward FDI:** domestic entities invest abroad.
- **Inward FDI:** foreign entities invest domestically.
- **Offshoring:** a subset where production moves abroad.

Not all FDI is offshoring.

## Trade Restrictions

Governments can restrict trade through:
- tariffs;
- quotas;
- subsidies;
- regulations.

## Limited Rationales for Intervention

The course treats legitimate cases as narrow, including industries with positive spillovers or strategic importance, and warns against turning these into general protectionist arguments.

## Takeaway

Trade can enlarge the economic pie while producing identifiable losers. That distinction explains much of the political difficulty around trade policy.
