# Monetary Policy

The monetary-policy section covers money, bank reserves, the Fed, interest-rate transmission, inflation, and countercyclical stabilization.

## Money

Money helps solve barter's double-coincidence-of-wants problem.

Types discussed:
- commodity;
- paper;
- fiat.

## Monetary Aggregates

`M = C + D`

where:
- `C` = currency;
- `D` = deposits.

Monetary base:

`H = C + R`

where `R` = bank reserves.

## Money Multiplier

The slides derive:

`M/H = (1 + c) / (c + rr)`

where:
- `c = C/D`;
- `rr = R/D`.

A higher reserve ratio lowers the multiplier.

The Great Recession example shows that a large increase in the monetary base need not create a proportional increase in broad money if reserves rise sharply.

## Central-Bank Tools

Traditional:
- open-market operations;
- discount window;
- reserve requirements.

Post-crisis / innovative:
- interest on reserves;
- quantitative easing;
- forward guidance.

## Federal Funds Rate

The federal funds market is the market for overnight reserve lending among banks.

The Fed directly influences the policy rate / target range, but household and firm spending depends more broadly on market yields, especially longer-maturity real yields.

## Transmission Mechanism

`Policy Rate → Market Yields → Consumption & Investment → Aggregate Demand → Output & Prices`

## Fed Objectives

The course emphasizes:
- price stability;
- maximum employment.

These goals can conflict.

## Monetary Tightening

A tightening:
- raises interest rates;
- reduces C and I;
- shifts AD left;
- lowers output and employment in the short run;
- lowers inflation pressure.

Thus, disinflation can come at the cost of recession.

## Liquidity Trap

Near the effective lower bound, conventional rate cuts have limited room, motivating tools such as QE and forward guidance.

## Taylor Rule

`i_t = r* + π_t + a1(π_t − π*) + a2(log Y_t − log Y*)`

Interpretation:
- inflation above target → higher policy rate;
- output above potential → higher policy rate;
- inflation/output below target → lower policy rate.

The rule is descriptive, not a mechanical law.

## Homework Application: Fed Asset Purchases

When the Fed buys an asset from a non-bank:
1. the seller's bank receives reserve credit;
2. the bank credits the seller's deposit;
3. system-wide reserves rise by the purchase amount.

Large excess reserves can coexist with lending; banks can transfer reserves among themselves, but the banking system cannot collectively eliminate Fed-created reserve balances simply by lending to non-banks.
