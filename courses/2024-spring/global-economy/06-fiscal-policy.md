# Fiscal Policy

Fiscal policy changes government purchases, transfers, taxes, and debt issuance.

## Government Budget Constraint

Simplified class constraint:

`Transfers + Government Purchases + Interest on Debt = Taxes + New Debt`

Government activity must ultimately be financed through taxation, borrowing, or—when the public sector is consolidated with the central bank—money creation.

## Government Purchases

An increase in government purchases raises aggregate demand.

Short-run consequences can include:
- output ↑;
- prices ↑;
- interest rates ↑.

## Crowding Out

### Consumption
Households may reduce consumption because higher government spending can imply lower lifetime private resources. The effect should be larger when the spending increase is permanent.

### Investment
Higher interest rates can reduce private investment.

## Medium Run

In the course model, a permanent increase in government spending does not permanently raise output above potential. It can instead produce:
- more government spending;
- lower private consumption;
- lower private investment;
- a higher price level.

## Transfers

Transfers are not themselves counted in GDP. They affect GDP when recipients spend them on final goods and services.

## Taxes

### Distortionary
Can change behavior through substitution effects.

### Lump-Sum / Non-Distortionary
Do not directly alter marginal work/saving incentives in the same way.

## Tax Rebates vs. Tax Cuts

Temporary rebates and persistent rate changes can have different effects because households respond to expected lifetime resources.

## Laffer Curve

My homework explains the conceptual Laffer Curve:
- 0% tax rate → zero revenue;
- very high rates can shrink the tax base;
- a revenue-maximizing tax rate exists somewhere in between.

A tax cut raises revenue only if the starting rate lies above that revenue-maximizing point. The curve does **not** imply that every tax cut pays for itself.

## Countercyclical Fiscal Policy

During a demand-driven recession, fiscal expansion can accelerate recovery.

Tradeoffs include:
- inflation;
- debt;
- crowding out;
- implementation timing.
