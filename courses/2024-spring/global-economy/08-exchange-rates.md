# Exchange Rates

The exchange-rate topic covers nominal rates, real rates, exchange-rate regimes, long-run anchors, and policy tradeoffs.

## Convention Warning

The course materials discuss both quote conventions.

The textbook's symbol table uses:

> `e = home-currency price of foreign currency`

Under that convention:
- `e ↑` = domestic-currency depreciation;
- `e ↓` = domestic-currency appreciation.

My class notes also contain examples phrased as "how much foreign currency does $1 buy?", which is the reciprocal convention.

**Always write the units before interpreting appreciation/depreciation.**

## Nominal Exchange Rate

The nominal exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another.

Under flexible rates:
- appreciation;
- depreciation.

Under fixed rates:
- revaluation;
- devaluation.

## Real Exchange Rate

Textbook convention:

`RER = e × P* / P`

where:
- `e` = home-currency price of foreign currency;
- `P*` = foreign price level;
- `P` = domestic price level.

## Trade Effects

Exchange-rate movements affect export competitiveness, import prices, and aggregate demand.

Quantities can respond more slowly than prices, and the course connects eventual net-export adjustment to the Marshall-Lerner condition.

## Exchange-Rate Regimes

### Floating
Market-determined rate.

**Advantage:** monetary-policy flexibility.  
**Disadvantage:** volatility.

### Managed Float
Central-bank intervention around a target zone/band.

### Fixed / Pegged
Central bank commits to a target rate.

**Advantage:** stability and predictability.  
**Disadvantage:** reduced independent monetary policy and vulnerability to pressure on the peg.

### Monetary Union
Members share a currency/monetary authority.

**Advantage:** no intra-union exchange-rate risk.  
**Disadvantage:** no independent member-level monetary policy.

### Currency Abandonment / Dollarization
A country uses another central bank's currency.

**Advantage:** exchange-rate stability against issuer.  
**Disadvantage:** no independent monetary policy or domestic seigniorage.

## Law of One Price

A tradable good should sell for the same converted price across locations when arbitrage is easy.

More likely to hold when:
- the good is tradable;
- transport costs are low;
- products are close substitutes;
- barriers are limited.

## Purchasing Power Parity

PPP extends the law-of-one-price intuition to broader price levels.

Long-run implication:

> persistently higher inflation should be associated with currency depreciation relative to lower-inflation countries.

PPP is a long-run anchor, not a strong short-run forecasting rule.

## Policy Trilemma

The textbook framework emphasizes a tradeoff among:
- fixed exchange rates;
- free capital mobility;
- independent monetary policy.

A country cannot fully maximize all three at once.
