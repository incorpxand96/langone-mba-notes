# Labor Markets

## Population Categories

Distinguish:
- total population;
- civilian non-institutional / working-age population;
- labor force;
- employment;
- unemployment;
- out of the labor force.

A full-time student who is not working and not looking for work is out of the labor force, not unemployed.

## Main Indicators

`Unemployment Rate = Unemployed / Labor Force`

`Labor-Force Participation = Labor Force / Working-Age Population`

`Employment-Population Ratio = Employment / Working-Age Population`

These measures answer different questions. A falling unemployment rate can coexist with weakness if participation also falls.

## U.S. Trends Discussed

- slower employment growth since the 1980s;
- slower population growth;
- lower labor-force participation since around 2000;
- larger older population share;
- lower participation among younger workers.

## Frictionless Model

Labor demand slopes down in the real wage; labor supply slopes up.

At equilibrium, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.

## Frictions

Real labor markets include:
- search/matching frictions;
- institutional frictions.

These help explain why vacancies and unemployment coexist.

## Wage Floors

A binding minimum wage sits above the market-clearing wage, so labor supplied exceeds labor demanded.

My homework emphasizes that an existing minimum wage only becomes binding after demand falls far enough that the new market-clearing wage would otherwise be below the floor.

## Labor-Market Policies

### Employment Laws
Minimum wage, hours, severance, layoffs, contracts.

### Industrial-Relations Laws
Bargaining, unions, collective agreements.

### Social-Security Laws
Old age, disability, unemployment, maternity.

## Labor-Shortage Application

My homework discussed demographic changes, pandemic-era reallocation, lower immigration, and education/work choices as possible sources of tightness.

Possible responses included flexible work for older workers, immigration reform, training/reskilling, and employer-training incentives.
