# Compelling Visuals

The course frames visual design as a three-step process:

1. **Create the storyboard**
2. **Choose the slide type**
3. **Coordinate visual elements**

---

# 1. Create the Storyboard

## One Idea Per Slide

My notes sharpen this to:

> **One idea / one "so what?" per slide.**

A slide should not merely have one topic.

It should make one primary point.

## Use Message Titles

The title should state the conclusion or message.

Example:

Weak:

> Sales

Stronger:

> Sales hit a new peak in 2019

Message titles should tell the story even if someone reads only the titles.

## Add Visual Representations

Use small graphics, images, or data displays to reinforce meaning.

---

# 2. Choose the Slide Type

Possible types:

- title;
- agenda;
- text;
- picture;
- chart;
- diagram.

Use the slide type that best supports the message.

### Pictures

Good for emotional or conceptual meaning.

### Charts

Good for quantitative claims.

### Diagrams

Good for qualitative relationships and processes.

---

# 3. Coordinate Visual Elements

## Contrast

Focus attention.

## Color

Enhance the message rather than decorate randomly.

## Alignment

Make the page easier to process.

## Readability

The audience should be able to grasp the slide quickly.

## Clarity

Simplify and streamline.

---

# Presentation vs. Preread

My notes add an important situational distinction.

### Live Persuasion

Use less text because the speaker carries more meaning.

### Preread / Leave-Behind

More content may be necessary because the slide must stand alone.

The correct level of density depends on how the material will be used.

---

# My Take

A slide is not a document page with a projector attached.

For live communication, the slide's job is usually to help the audience:

- orient;
- see;
- remember.

The title often does more strategic work than the graphic.

---

# Rules of Thumb

- One primary "so what?" per slide.
- Use message titles.
- Build the storyboard before polishing.
- Choose the right visual form.
- Let charts make claims, not merely show data.
- Use whitespace deliberately.
- Adapt density to the use case.
- Never make the audience choose between reading and listening.
