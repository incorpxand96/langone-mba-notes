# GM and Self-Disruption

GM's electric-vehicle architecture provides a clean example of why incumbent self-disruption can be economically unattractive even when the new technology looks promising.

## Existing Auto Hierarchy

Internal-combustion vehicles depended on:

- thousands of parts;
- deep mechanical-engineering capability;
- large supplier hierarchies;
- physical infrastructure;
- specialized employees;
- decades of accumulated coordination.

## EV Architecture

Electric vehicles can require:

- far fewer parts;
- more software capability;
- a different supplier ecosystem;
- different production knowledge.

## Incumbent Problem

The incumbent does not compare:

`new technology vs. nothing`

It compares:

`new technology vs. continued cash flow from already-paid-for assets/capabilities`.

## Sunk-Cost Effect

A new EV program can accelerate the economic depreciation of valuable existing assets.

## Replacement Effect

GM replacing its existing franchise with a new GM franchise can create less incremental value than a startup moving from zero to meaningful share.

## Efficiency Effect

If credible entrants will capture the new market anyway, the economics change.

Failing to invest may sacrifice monopoly/market power faster than self-disruption would.

## Principle

> **Self-disruption is not automatically rational; it becomes rational when the cost of accommodation exceeds the cost of cannibalization.**
