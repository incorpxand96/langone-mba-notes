# Disney, Pixar, and DreamWorks

This comparison is especially useful for understanding why creative businesses depend on more than access to money and famous talent.

## Pixar

Pixar combined:

- proprietary technology;
- production-management systems;
- creative culture;
- concentrated talent;
- disciplined film pipeline;
- strong relationship with Disney distribution.

Steve Jobs' long investment horizon allowed the company to survive while these capabilities accumulated.

## DreamWorks

DreamWorks assembled enormous talent and capital, but talent acquisition alone did not reproduce Pixar's economics.

The timeline invites a useful question:

> Why did Jobs' investment in Pixar create so much more value than Paul Allen's investment in DreamWorks?

Possible explanations include:

- stronger organizational architecture;
- more coherent technology/creative process;
- superior IP hit rate;
- lower relative production economics;
- more effective complementarity with Disney.

## Disney-Pixar Relationship

The relationship began as mutually valuable:

- Pixar supplied creative/CG capability;
- Disney supplied financing, distribution, marketing, and exploitation of IP.

As Pixar's bargaining power increased, the distribution of value became more contentious.

This is a classic distinction between:

- value creation;
- value appropriation.

## Talent Mobility and IP

The Levandowski/Google material broadens the issue beyond entertainment: innovative capacity often resides partly in people who can leave.

That makes ownership of:

- formal IP;
- organizational routines;
- team relationships;
- tacit knowledge
strategically important but difficult to separate cleanly.

## Principle

> **In creative and technical businesses, the scarce asset is often not an individual star but the system that repeatedly turns talent into valuable output.**
