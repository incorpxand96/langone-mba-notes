# Defensibility, Preemption, and Scarcity

A WTP-C advantage matters only if competitors cannot **profitably** copy it.

The key word is profitably.

## Preemption

The incumbent invests in tangible and intangible assets so extensively that the residual market left to an imitator is too small to justify the required investment.

This is a microeconomic account of a moat.

## Six Conditions That Affect Defensibility

A position is generally harder to defend when:

1. scale requirements are low;
2. the product is easy to evaluate before purchase;
3. experimentation with alternatives is low risk;
4. the product is a large share of the buyer's budget, motivating search;
5. innovation requirements are low;
6. network effects are absent.

The inverse conditions tend to strengthen defensibility.

## Information Is the Enemy of Differentiation

If buyers can easily compare products and verify attributes, price competition intensifies.

Information frictions can preserve differentiation when customers cannot cheaply confirm equivalence.

## Experimentation Risk

Customers may remain with a familiar provider when trying an alternative risks:

- failure;
- downtime;
- safety;
- reputation;
- switching costs.

## Low Share of Budget

When the product is inexpensive relative to the buyer's budget, the upside from extensive search can be small.

This can reduce shopping intensity.

## Network Effects

If users benefit from being on the same network as other users, an incumbent's position can become self-reinforcing.

## Principle

> **A moat is not that imitation is impossible; it is that imitation is unattractive relative to the residual prize.**
