# Working Capital and the Cash Conversion Cycle

Operations and finance meet directly in working capital.

The cash conversion cycle measures how long the company must finance its operating cycle before recovering cash from customers.

---

# Timeline

A simplified sequence:

```text
Receive inventory → Pay supplier → Sell product → Collect customer cash
```

Three time measures:

- DIO — Days Inventory Outstanding
- DSO — Days Sales Outstanding
- DPO — Days Payable Outstanding

---

# Cash Conversion Cycle

`CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO`

A longer CCC generally means more cash is tied up in operations.

A negative CCC means the firm can collect from customers before paying suppliers.

---

# Little's Law Interpretation

## DIO

System:

inventory held for sale.

- `I = Inventory`
- `R = COGS per day`
- `T = DIO`

Therefore:

`DIO = Inventory / COGS per day`

## DSO

System:

receivables waiting for collection.

`DSO = Accounts Receivable / Sales per day`

## DPO

System:

supplier obligations waiting for payment.

Conceptually:

`DPO = Accounts Payable / Purchases per day`

COGS is often used as an approximation when purchase data are unavailable.

---

# Why CCC Matters

CCC links operating choices to:

- liquidity;
- external funding need;
- cash flow;
- return on capital.

Operational levers include:

- faster production;
- lower inventory;
- faster sell-through;
- tighter collection;
- supplier payment terms.

---

# Do Not Optimize Blindly

Lower CCC is usually attractive, but each lever has tradeoffs.

Examples:

- less inventory can create stockouts;
- tight customer terms can reduce demand;
- slower supplier payment can increase supplier prices or strain relationships.

The objective is economic optimization, not merely minimizing days.

---

# Apple

Apple illustrates an unusually powerful operating-finance model:

- low DIO;
- relatively low DSO;
- very high DPO.

This can create a negative CCC.

Economically, customers and suppliers can partly finance operations.

# Accounting Connection

This directly overlaps with Financial Accounting & Reporting.

Accounting tells us where:

- inventory;
- receivables;
- payables

sit on the balance sheet.

Operations explains what process design creates those balances.
