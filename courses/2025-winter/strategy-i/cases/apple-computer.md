# Apple Computer

## Strategic Problem

Apple possessed a differentiated product but struggled to sustain advantage in the personal-computer industry.

The case asks why.

---

# Industry Pressures

Macs remained exposed to PC-industry economics:

- falling hardware prices;
- strengthening Windows substitutes;
- high OS/software development economics;
- an enormous installed-base disadvantage;
- weaker complements/software availability.

---

# Product Advantage vs. Competitive Advantage

One of the course's clearest lessons:

> **A product advantage is not the same thing as a competitive advantage.**

A strong product can fail strategically if the surrounding value system is weak.

Relevant factors include:

- complements;
- standards;
- installed base;
- cost structure;
- distribution;
- ecosystem.

---

# Standards

Competing standards can coexist when they remain meaningfully differentiated.

As differences narrow, maintaining separate standards becomes harder to justify.

Windows' installed-base momentum strengthened its complement advantage.

---

# Timing

Strategic options change with time.

A move that might have been viable in the late 1980s could become ineffective after an ecosystem tips.

This matters for:

- licensing;
- standards;
- platform strategy;
- entry.

---

# Apple's Response

Jobs' strategy included:

- focus on loyal users;
- premium positioning;
- design;
- retail;
- iMac;
- later Intel transition.

These improved the position but did not by themselves erase unfavorable PC-industry structure.

The iPod and later diversification changed the strategic arena.

---

# Durable Lesson

> **Analyze the economics of the entire value system, not merely whether customers prefer the focal product.**
