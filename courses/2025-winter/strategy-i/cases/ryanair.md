# Ryanair

Ryanair is both a rivalry case and a positioning case.

---

# Initial Entry

Ryanair entered Dublin–London with:

- substantially lower price;
- small aircraft;
- but full-service features and amenities.

This created a dangerous mismatch:

> low price without a clearly low-cost activity system.

---

# Incumbent Context

British Airways and Aer Lingus had:

- established brands;
- scale;
- route positions;
- entrenched customer bases.

They also carried:

- high labor costs;
- legacy operations;
- structural inefficiencies.

The strategic question was whether they could or would respond aggressively.

---

# What Went Wrong

The early position resembled incumbent choices too closely.

That left Ryanair vulnerable because its low fare lacked enough structural cost support.

---

# Turnaround

Under Michael O'Leary, Ryanair became a genuinely different system:

- dropped loss-making routes;
- redeployed aircraft;
- eliminated frills;
- focused on cash;
- cut cost aggressively;
- lowered fares;
- added ancillary revenue.

This was a strategic position rather than a price promotion.

---

# Rival Response

Legacy carriers found it difficult to match fares without damaging their own economics.

They increasingly emphasized higher-fare business customers.

Tradeoffs made imitation costly.

---

# Market Expansion

A powerful low-cost position can:

- steal share;
- **and expand the market**

by bringing customers from substitute modes such as rail/ferry into air travel.

---

# Durable Lesson

> **A low-price strategy is sustainable only when supported by an activity system that makes low cost structurally credible.**
