# Competitive Positioning and the WTP–C Wedge

The central question shifts from:

> Why are some industries more attractive?

to:

> **Why are some firms within the same industry more profitable than others?**

---

# Value Creation

A useful strategy abstraction is:

`Value Created = Willingness to Pay − Cost`

or:

`WTP − C`

The larger the wedge, the more total economic value exists to divide among:

- customers;
- the firm;
- suppliers / employees / other value-chain participants.

---

# Differentiation

Differentiation attempts to raise:

`WTP`

through attributes customers value.

Possible drivers:

- quality;
- brand;
- convenience;
- service;
- experience;
- speed;
- design.

But:

> **Higher WTP does not automatically mean a better strategy.**

If the cost of creating that WTP rises by more than the WTP increase, the wedge shrinks.

---

# Low Cost

A low-cost strategy attempts to lower `C` while maintaining enough customer value to sustain demand.

Low cost does not mean "cheap" in a simplistic sense.

It means a superior activity system produces a lower economic cost structure.

---

# Value Capture

Price determines how the WTP–C wedge is divided.

A firm can create substantial value yet fail to capture much of it if:

- customers have bargaining power;
- suppliers capture the gains;
- rivalry forces price down;
- employees appropriate the value;
- imitation eliminates scarcity.

This links positioning back to Five Forces.

---

# Trader Joe's

Trader Joe's illustrates differentiated positioning with strong cost discipline.

Its system includes:

- limited SKUs;
- heavy private label;
- small stores;
- direct purchasing;
- high employee pay;
- unusual service/culture;
- minimal traditional advertising;
- rapid product rotation.

This is not simply "premium grocery."

It is a coherent configuration that raises WTP for a target customer while reducing costs in other areas.

---

# Stuck in the Middle

A firm can fail by carrying:

- differentiation-level costs;
- without enough WTP;

while also lacking:

- low-cost efficiency.

The course uses Supervalu as an example of weak strategic coherence.

---

# Shake Shack Lesson

A segment can love a product and the company can still lack strong competitive advantage.

Questions:

1. Are we investing in attributes customers weight heavily?
2. Do those investments raise WTP enough to cover their cost?
3. Is the target segment large enough to amortize fixed investments?
4. Can rivals profitably replicate the wedge?

---

# My Take

The WTP–C wedge forces strategic claims into economics.

Instead of saying:

> "Customers love us."

ask:

> **How much additional willingness-to-pay does this activity generate, what does it cost, who captures the resulting value, and how replicable is it?**
