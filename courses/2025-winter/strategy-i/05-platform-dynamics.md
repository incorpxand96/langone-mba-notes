# Platform Dynamics and Competition

A platform creates value by facilitating exchanges among groups.

Examples:

- buyers and merchants;
- users and advertisers;
- developers and players;
- drivers and riders.

The platform supplies:

- infrastructure;
- rules;
- mechanisms for interaction.

---

# Traditional vs. Platform Model

## Traditional

The firm:

- purchases inputs;
- transforms them;
- sells output to customers.

## Platform

The firm enables interaction among external groups.

Much of the value creation occurs **between users** rather than inside the firm.

---

# Transaction Costs

A platform works only if it meaningfully improves exchange.

It can reduce:

- search costs;
- matching costs;
- trust/enforcement costs;
- payment friction;
- coordination costs.

The course emphasizes that many early B2B platforms failed because they did not materially lower transaction costs.

---

# Network Effects

## Cross-Side

Value to one side rises with the quantity or quality of users on the other side.

Example:

- more players attract developers;
- more games attract players.

## Same-Side

More users on the same side can have:

- positive effects;
- negative congestion/competition effects.

---

# Why Network Effects Are Different

Conventional scale:

> volume rises → unit cost falls.

Network scale:

> volume rises → **customer value can rise too.**

This creates increasing returns.

---

# Platform Pricing

The objective is not symmetric pricing.

The platform asks:

> **Which pricing structure grows total network value?**

Possible logic:

- subsidize the more price-sensitive side;
- charge the side whose demand increases most when the other side grows;
- subsidize marquee users;
- use pricing to screen for quality;
- charge upon successful transaction rather than access.

The side receiving subsidy can even be free.

---

# Chicken-and-Egg Problem

Each side wants the other side to exist first.

Early platform strategy may require:

- subsidies;
- exclusivity;
- marquee users;
- pre-existing relationships;
- investment ahead of revenue.

---

# Winner-Take-All

A market is more likely to tip when:

1. network effects are strong;
2. multi-homing costs are high for at least one side;
3. users do not require strong differentiated features.

Past success then reinforces future advantage.

---

# First-Mover Advantage

First mover helps but is not sufficient.

Late entrants may:

- learn from pioneer errors;
- use superior technology;
- choose a better position.

Winner-take-all dynamics still require a defensible underlying advantage.

---

# Envelopment

An adjacent platform can enter by leveraging overlapping users and bundling functionality.

This blurs market boundaries.

A platform should therefore monitor not only direct rivals but also adjacent ecosystems.

---

# Platform Viability Test

Before calling something a platform opportunity, ask:

1. Are there distinct user groups?
2. Does facilitating interaction create meaningful value?
3. Are transaction costs materially reduced?
4. Are network effects meaningful?
5. Can the platform capture value without destroying participation?
6. Is disintermediation manageable?
7. Can the economics scale sustainably?

---

# My Take

A platform is not simply a digital marketplace.

The strategic essence is:

> **design an interaction system whose value increases as the right participants join, while capturing enough of that value to sustain the system.**
