# My Ryanair Scenario Analysis

This note preserves my submitted Ryanair analysis and the strategic thinking behind it.

---

# Core Question

I modeled two strategic decisions:

## Ryanair

- maintain advertised `I£98` fare;
- preemptively lower fare to `I£90`.

## British Airways + Aer Lingus

- do not retaliate;
- cut price through cost reductions and margin sacrifice.

This creates four scenarios.

---

# Key Assumptions

For Ryanair:

- 8 flights/day;
- 44 seats/flight;
- 352 seats/day;
- 128,480 maximum annual seats.

For BA + Aer Lingus:

- 12 flights/day;
- average 175 seats;
- 766,500 maximum annual seats.

For incumbent retaliation, I assumed meaningful cost reductions and a lower margin, producing an average retaliatory fare around `I£150.51`.

The analysis explicitly acknowledges uncertainty around:

- price elasticity;
- Ryanair's cost structure;
- incumbent ability to reduce cost.

That caveat is important.

---

# Scenario 1

**Ryanair advertised fare; no incumbent retaliation.**

Assumptions/results:

- Ryanair 95% load;
- converts roughly 15% of incumbent passengers;
- expands the market with former rail/ferry customers;
- incumbent revenue falls roughly 15%.

This produced the highest total market revenue among my modeled entry scenarios.

---

# Scenario 2

**Ryanair advertised fare; incumbents retaliate.**

Assumptions/results:

- Ryanair load falls to 90%;
- incumbent passenger attrition is reduced;
- incumbents still suffer greater revenue pressure because they lower fare on retained passengers.

This highlights an important rivalry insight:

> retaliation can protect share while still destroying value.

---

# Scenario 3

**Ryanair preemptively lowers fare; no retaliation.**

Assumptions/results:

- Ryanair reaches 100% load;
- lower price reduces Ryanair revenue versus Scenario 1;
- incumbents gain little incremental damage versus the already attractive advertised fare.

This questions the logic of preemptive price cutting when the initial offer is already disruptive.

---

# Scenario 4

**Ryanair lowers fare; incumbents retaliate.**

This produces Ryanair's lowest modeled revenue.

Both sides sacrifice price.

---

# My Strategic Interpretation

The analysis demonstrates why rivalry is an interaction problem rather than a single-firm forecast.

But it also exposes a limitation:

> Revenue scenarios alone cannot identify the best strategy without knowing cost structures and profit consequences.

The decisive strategic question is therefore:

> **Which player can sustain the price architecture because its underlying activity system makes the economics credible?**

That is exactly where the later Ryanair turnaround becomes more strategically meaningful than the original launch.

---

# What I Would Carry Forward

When analyzing a competitive move:

1. model rival response;
2. state assumptions explicitly;
3. separate share from profit;
4. consider market expansion, not only share transfer;
5. test whether price changes are supported by cost structure;
6. distinguish credible from value-destructive retaliation.
