# Communication and Interpretation

## Definition

Organizational communication is not simply the transmission of information from one person to another.

A message can be:

1. **sent**;
2. **received**;
3. **perceived**; and
4. **interpreted**.

The fact that I said something does not mean the other person heard the same thing I intended.

That gap between **message sent** and **meaning constructed** is one of the basic sources of organizational conflict.

---

## Why It Matters

Managers often treat communication failure as a problem of insufficient clarity:

> "I already told them."

But communication can fail even when the words themselves are clear.

People interpret messages through:

- incentives;
- prior relationships;
- expectations;
- status;
- emotion;
- trust;
- perceived fairness;
- timing; and
- surrounding organizational context.

The practical unit of communication is therefore not the sentence I deliver.

It is the **meaning the other person takes away**.

---

## Framework

When a consequential message does not land as intended, I want to diagnose four stages.

### 1. Sent

What did I actually communicate?

Questions:

- What words did I use?
- What channel did I choose?
- What tone did I use?
- What did I leave unstated?
- Did my behavior contradict my words?

### 2. Received

Did the message actually reach the person in a usable form?

Questions:

- Did they hear or read it?
- Was attention divided?
- Was the medium appropriate?
- Was key information buried?
- Did timing reduce attention?

### 3. Perceived

What elements of the message became salient?

Two people can receive the same information and notice different things.

A recipient may focus on:

- a number;
- a concession;
- criticism;
- threat;
- change in tone;
- status signal;
- omission.

### 4. Interpreted

What meaning did the recipient assign?

Examples:

- "They are giving me useful feedback."
- "They do not trust me."
- "This is a threat."
- "There is room to negotiate."
- "They have already made up their mind."

Interpretation converts information into action.

---

## Listening Is an Information Tool

The course treats listening as both an interpersonal skill and a strategic one.

Good listening can reveal:

- interests;
- constraints;
- emotional reactions;
- priorities;
- misunderstanding;
- hidden conflict.

The point is not simply to make the other person feel heard.

Listening changes the information available for the next decision.

---

## Rephrasing and Confirmation

One practical defense against interpretation error is to test understanding.

Useful moves include:

- summarize what I think I heard;
- ask the other person to react;
- distinguish what they said from what I inferred;
- clarify important assumptions;
- separate facts from interpretations.

For example:

> "I hear that timing is the main issue, rather than the amount itself. Is that right?"

This creates an opportunity to correct a mistaken interpretation before it hardens into conflict.

---

## Communication Under Conflict

Conflict changes how messages are processed.

When trust is low or emotion is high:

- ambiguous messages are more easily read negatively;
- concessions may be interpreted as weakness;
- questions may be interpreted as challenges;
- silence may be interpreted as hostility;
- explanations may be interpreted as excuses.

This means the same words can produce different effects depending on relationship context.

---

## Information Sharing Is Not Binary

Collaboration does not require complete transparency.

Different information has different strategic value.

### Often Useful to Share

- underlying interests;
- priorities;
- constraints;
- areas of flexibility;
- relevant factual information;
- trade-offs.

### Often Risky to Share Automatically

- reservation points;
- weak alternatives;
- desperation;
- private information that eliminates bargaining leverage.

The challenge is not "be open" or "be guarded."

It is:

> **Share the information that allows coordination while protecting information whose disclosure would unnecessarily weaken my position.**

---

## Symbolic Communication

Messages have symbolic as well as substantive content.

A small action can communicate:

- respect;
- status;
- seriousness;
- reciprocity;
- distrust;
- willingness to collaborate.

For example, the substantive value of a concession may matter less than whether the other party perceives that I made one.

This is why concessions should often be **explained rather than silently given**.

---

## Communication and Fairness

People evaluate not only outcomes but also process.

A difficult decision can be easier to accept when people believe:

- they were heard;
- the process was consistent;
- reasons were explained;
- they were treated respectfully.

Conversely, even an economically reasonable outcome may generate conflict when communication makes the process feel arbitrary or dismissive.

---

## How I Think About It

I increasingly think a manager's communication responsibility should extend beyond:

> "Was I clear?"

to:

> **"What interpretation was reasonably likely given the other person's incentives, emotions, and context?"**

This does not mean I can control how everyone interprets every message.

It means communication is incomplete until I have considered the receiver's perspective.

Especially for important decisions, I should design communication for the **recipient's likely interpretation**, not merely for my own sense of clarity.

---

## Rules of Thumb

- Communication is complete only when meaning is understood.
- Separate what I said from what they inferred.
- Listen for interests, not just words.
- Rephrase important points to test understanding.
- Choose channel and timing deliberately.
- Explain concessions so they are perceived as concessions.
- Share priorities more freely than bottom lines.
- Expect emotion and trust to affect interpretation.
- In recurring relationships, process matters alongside substance.
- Before escalating a conflict, check for interpretation error.

---

## Example: Leckenby

The Leckenby exercise deliberately limited ordinary communication and then highlighted the sequence:

> **sent → received → perceived → interpreted**

It also paired communication with leverage, reciprocity, fairness, and concession making.

The broader lesson is that bargaining behavior itself communicates.

A concession can be interpreted as:

- goodwill;
- weakness;
- reciprocity;
- desperation;
- movement toward a limit.

The negotiator therefore needs to manage both **economic movement** and **the meaning attached to that movement**.

---

## Failure Modes

### "I Already Told Them"

Treating transmission as understanding.

### Over-Explaining

Adding more words without resolving the interpretive problem.

### Assuming Intent Equals Impact

Believing that benign intent guarantees benign interpretation.

### Withholding Too Much

Protecting information so aggressively that collaboration becomes impossible.

### Disclosing Too Much

Confusing openness with giving away strategic information.

### Ignoring the Channel

Using email, chat, or a group meeting for a message that requires richer two-way communication.

### Communicating While Emotional

Allowing anger or defensiveness to distort both the sent message and the interpretation.
