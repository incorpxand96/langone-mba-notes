# Organizational Conflict

## Definition

Organizational conflict occurs when people or groups perceive that their goals, interests, identities, resources, or preferred actions are incompatible.

Conflict is not automatically a sign that an organization is malfunctioning.

It may reveal real differences in:

- incentives;
- information;
- priorities;
- time horizons;
- risk tolerance;
- constituencies;
- values;
- power.

The managerial question is therefore not:

> **How do I eliminate conflict?**

It is:

> **What kind of conflict is this, what is producing it, and what process is most likely to make it productive?**

---

## Why It Matters

Organizations require coordination among people who do not have identical goals.

Conflict is therefore structural, not exceptional.

Attempting to suppress all conflict can create:

- false consensus;
- hidden resistance;
- poor information;
- weak decisions;
- resentment;
- coalition behavior outside formal meetings.

But unmanaged conflict can create:

- delay;
- distrust;
- political warfare;
- escalation;
- damaged relationships;
- implementation failure.

The objective is **productive conflict**: enough disagreement to expose important differences, with enough process and trust to reach action.

---

## A Diagnostic Framework

Before choosing a conflict-management approach, diagnose the source.

### 1. Substantive Conflict

The parties genuinely prefer different outcomes.

Examples:

- budget allocation;
- compensation;
- strategic priorities;
- headcount;
- pricing;
- project selection.

This may require bargaining, objective criteria, or explicit trade-offs.

### 2. Interest Conflict

The parties care about different underlying needs.

A stated disagreement may mask different interests such as:

- speed vs. quality;
- certainty vs. upside;
- control vs. autonomy;
- short-term performance vs. long-term investment.

This creates potential for integrative solutions.

### 3. Information Conflict

The parties disagree because they possess different facts, forecasts, or interpretations.

Possible responses:

- gather data;
- clarify assumptions;
- test forecasts;
- use contingent decisions;
- improve communication.

### 4. Relationship Conflict

The substantive issue becomes entangled with:

- trust;
- identity;
- respect;
- history;
- perceived fairness;
- status.

Purely economic solutions may not resolve this.

### 5. Structural Conflict

The organization itself creates incompatible incentives or authority.

Examples:

- two functions measured against conflicting KPIs;
- unclear decision rights;
- shared resources without allocation rules;
- veto power;
- misaligned reporting lines.

The solution may require redesign rather than better interpersonal behavior.

### 6. Political / Coalitional Conflict

Groups organize around aligned interests.

Power depends on:

- coalition membership;
- vetoes;
- decision rules;
- constituencies;
- informal relationships.

This is especially important in cross-functional and multi-party settings.

## Conflict Style Should Follow Diagnosis

The course's five styles are useful only when connected to context.

### Compete

Useful when:

- stakes are high;
- a clear decision is required;
- protecting a core interest matters.

### Collaborate

Useful when:

- stakes are high;
- relationship matters;
- multiple issues or interests create potential trades.

### Compromise

Useful when:

- time matters;
- an adequate middle ground is more valuable than an exhaustive search.

### Avoid

Useful when:

- emotion is temporarily too high;
- issue value is low;
- more information is needed;
- timing will improve the conversation.

### Accommodate

Useful when:

- the relationship matters more than the issue;
- preserving goodwill has strategic value;
- I am wrong or the other side's need is legitimately more important.

No style is universally correct.

---

## Conflict and Goals

A useful way to choose behavior is to consider three categories of goals.

### Substantive Goals

What outcome do I need?

How large are the stakes?

### Relational Goals

What relationship do I want after this?

Will we work together again?

### Temporal Goals

Is the issue:

- urgent;
- repeated;
- one-shot;
- long-term;
- reversible?

Conflict strategy should reflect all three.

---

## Conflict and Power

Conflict is rarely independent of power.

Power may come from:

- formal authority;
- control of resources;
- expertise;
- information;
- BATNA;
- relationships;
- coalition support;
- veto rights.

A manager should therefore distinguish:

> **Who has formal authority?**

from:

> **Who can actually block, delay, or reshape the outcome?**

---

## Organizational Politics and Coalitions

The Harborco exercise highlights how group conflict becomes political.

With multiple parties:

- coalitions form;
- counter-coalitions form;
- hidden agendas matter;
- constituencies constrain negotiators;
- decision rules shape leverage;
- vetoes can dominate the process.

This does not mean organizational politics is inherently pathological.

Coalition formation is often a rational response to dispersed interests and power.

The managerial task is to understand the political structure rather than pretend it does not exist.

---

## Conformity and False Consensus

Groups introduce a second danger: excessive agreement.

Pressure to conform can cause people to:

- suppress reservations;
- forget their own minimum requirements;
- accept majority preference as automatically correct;
- prioritize group momentum over analysis.

A healthy conflict process should preserve space for dissent.

---

## Conflict Escalation

Conflict can become self-reinforcing.

A typical cycle:

1. I interpret their behavior negatively.
2. I respond more aggressively or defensively.
3. They interpret my response as confirmation.
4. They escalate.
5. The original issue becomes secondary to the relationship conflict.

This is one reason a pause can be strategically valuable.

Breaking the emotional cycle can restore the original problem to the center.

---

## Fairness and Conflict

People care about both:

- **what** they receive; and
- **how** the outcome was produced.

Perceived unfairness can intensify conflict even when the material stakes are modest.

Managers should therefore consider:

- consistency;
- explanation;
- voice;
- reciprocity;
- respect.

Fair process can increase acceptance of difficult outcomes.

---

## How I Think About It

I think the most common managerial mistake is treating conflict as a personality problem too quickly.

Sometimes it is interpersonal.

But often people are behaving rationally inside a poorly designed system.

Before asking:

> "Why are these people fighting?"

I want to ask:

> **"What incentives, information, authority, dependencies, or decision rules make this conflict predictable?"**

That shifts the manager from referee to system designer.

At the same time, structural diagnosis cannot replace interpersonal skill.

A well-designed system still contains humans who care about identity, fairness, status, and relationships.

---

## Rules of Thumb

- Diagnose before choosing a style.
- Do not assume disagreement is dysfunction.
- Distinguish positions from interests.
- Check whether incentives are producing the conflict.
- Clarify decision rights.
- Separate relationship conflict from substantive conflict where possible.
- Allow dissent before demanding alignment.
- Map informal power as well as formal authority.
- In multi-party conflict, map coalitions and veto players.
- Pause when emotion begins to dominate.
- Use fair process because implementation depends on legitimacy.
- Once a decision is made, distinguish healthy dissent during debate from resistance during execution.

---

## Example: Harborco

Harborco demonstrates that a group can contain many economically feasible agreements yet still fail to reach one.

Why?

Because the negotiation also contains:

- veto rights;
- different constituencies;
- coalitions;
- hidden agendas;
- strategic voting;
- pressure to conform;
- relationships among parties.

The case is a useful reminder that organizational conflict is often not solved by finding the mathematically "best" answer.

The answer must also be **politically and procedurally feasible**.

---

## Failure Modes

### Conflict Avoidance as Culture

People stop surfacing real disagreement.

### Premature Compromise

The group splits differences before understanding interests.

### Personalizing Structural Problems

Blaming individuals for behavior created by incentives or roles.

### Structuralizing Personal Problems

Assuming every trust or respect problem can be fixed by changing the org chart.

### Majority Rule by Reflex

Using a vote when the implementation requires support from parties who can later obstruct execution.

### Consensus as a Goal

Optimizing for agreement rather than decision quality.

### Winning the Argument, Losing the Organization

Securing the formal decision while destroying trust or implementation commitment.
