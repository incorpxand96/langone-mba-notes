# Multi-Party Negotiation and Coalitions

## Definition

Multi-party negotiation involves three or more parties whose agreement depends on formal decision rules, informal influence, coalitions, or some combination.

A coalition is a subset of participants that cooperates to influence the group outcome.

---

## Why It Matters

Adding parties introduces:

- additional preferences;
- additional relationships;
- coalition possibilities;
- vetoes;
- constituencies;
- conformity pressure;
- information complexity.

The best bilateral agreement may be irrelevant if the broader group will not approve it.

---

## Framework

Before negotiating, map:

### Parties

Who is actually involved?

### Decision Rights

Who votes?

Who can veto?

What majority is required?

### Interests

What does each party care about?

### Reservation Points

What minimum outcome will each require?

### Coalitions

Which combinations can control the outcome?

### Constituencies

Who must each representative satisfy outside the room?

### Relationships

Who trusts whom?

Who has obligations or history?

---

## Power

Power is often relational.

A smaller party can have significant power if:

- it is pivotal;
- it has veto authority;
- several winning coalitions need it;
- it can credibly defect.

This is why coalition value matters more than visible status alone.

---

## How I Think About It

Multi-party negotiation is partly negotiation and partly governance.

The decision rule changes the economics.

The process for reaching agreement therefore deserves as much attention as the substantive issues.

---

## Rules of Thumb

- Map vetoes first.
- Model coalitions.
- Remember constituencies.
- Recruit enough support to win, not automatically everyone.
- Expect counter-coalitions.
- Design team roles deliberately.
- Centralize information.
- Beware conformity.
- Preserve key relationships after the vote.

---

## Failure Modes

- Treating six-party negotiation as six bilateral negotiations.
- Forgetting a veto player.
- Ignoring the constituency behind a representative.
- Building an oversized unstable coalition.
- Letting group momentum override reservation points.
