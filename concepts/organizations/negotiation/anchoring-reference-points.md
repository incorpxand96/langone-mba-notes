# Anchoring and Reference Points

## Definition

**Anchoring** occurs when an initial number or reference point disproportionately influences later judgment.

A **reference point** is any comparison that shapes how an outcome is evaluated.

---

## Why It Matters

Negotiation is full of reference points:

- opening offers;
- list prices;
- prior prices;
- budgets;
- appraisals;
- market comparables;
- "fair" outcomes;
- other bids.

The party that establishes the frame can influence what feels reasonable.

---

## Framework

### Before the Negotiation

Prepare:

- reservation point;
- aspiration;
- market data;
- several favorable objective references;
- responses to likely unfavorable references.

### If I Am Informed

Consider making the first offer to control the anchor.

### If I Am Poorly Informed

Gather information before committing to a number.

### If They Anchor First

Do not merely argue within their number.

Re-anchor.

---

## How I Think About It

The danger of anchoring is not that I consciously believe the anchor.

It is that the anchor silently narrows the range I explore.

The practical defense is to create my own numbers before hearing theirs.

---

## Rules of Thumb

- Write down my range in advance.
- Make an ambitious but defensible opening when informed.
- Use evidence to support the anchor.
- Re-anchor quickly.
- Challenge the relevance of their reference point, not merely its magnitude.
- Never assume expertise eliminates anchoring bias.

---

## Failure Modes

### Countering Too Close

Responding to an extreme anchor with a modest move validates their range.

### Unjustified Extremes

An outrageous number without rationale can damage credibility.

### One Reference Point

Relying on a single comparable gives the other side an easy target.

### Reference-Point Confusion

Treating historical cost as economic value when it is no longer relevant.
