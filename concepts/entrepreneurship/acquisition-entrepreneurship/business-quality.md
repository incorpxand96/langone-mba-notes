# Business Quality

## Definition

**Business quality** is the degree to which a company's economic engine is durable, understandable, transferable, and capable of producing attractive cash flow without requiring unusually favorable conditions.

For an acquisition entrepreneur, business quality matters before valuation.

A cheap bad business may still be a bad acquisition.

---

## Why It Matters

Buying an existing company means buying a set of relationships and processes that already produce an outcome.

The buyer needs that system to keep functioning after:

- the seller leaves;
- acquisition debt is added;
- employees react to new ownership;
- suppliers and customers learn of the change; and
- the buyer inevitably makes mistakes.

A high-quality business gives the new owner room to be imperfect.

---

## Framework

I currently evaluate business quality across seven dimensions.

### 1. Revenue Durability

Questions:

- Is revenue recurring, contractual, or highly repeatable?
- Why do customers come back?
- How costly or inconvenient is switching?
- Is demand mission-critical or discretionary?
- Does the company need to "re-win" most of its revenue every year?

Repeat revenue without a reason for repetition is weaker than true structural recurrence.

### 2. Concentration

Look at both sides of the business.

**Customer concentration:** Can one customer's departure materially impair the company?

**Supplier concentration:** Can one vendor change economics, withhold supply, or disintermediate the company?

A relationship can simultaneously be a moat and a concentration risk.

### 3. Cash-Flow Quality

Strong EBITDA is more useful when it:

- converts to cash;
- does not require heavy maintenance capex;
- does not consume excessive working capital; and
- persists through ordinary economic volatility.

EBITDA should not be confused with cash available to the owner.

### 4. Competitive Position

Ask what a new competitor would have difficulty replicating.

Potential sources include:

- reputation;
- embedded workflows;
- licenses or approvals;
- customer relationships;
- supplier access;
- proprietary processes;
- distribution;
- domain expertise;
- location; and
- scale.

### 5. Transferability

The business must be able to survive a change in ownership.

Key questions:

- Does the owner hold the customer relationships?
- Does the owner perform essential technical work?
- Are processes documented?
- Is there a credible management layer?
- Do major contracts survive change of control?

### 6. Industry Structure

Prefer environments with some combination of:

- fragmentation;
- understandable demand;
- low disruption risk;
- modest cyclicality;
- manageable regulation; and
- credible long-term need for the product or service.

Industry growth is useful, but it should not compensate for weak company economics.

### 7. Operating Complexity

Complexity is not automatically bad.

But every additional dependency creates something the buyer must understand and manage:

- thousands of SKUs;
- specialized personnel;
- regulatory approvals;
- project-based revenue;
- volatile inventory;
- custom software; or
- complicated supplier arrangements.

Complexity should earn a return.

---

## How I Think About It

I increasingly think the defining feature of a good ETA business is **durability rather than excitement**.

Growth matters.

But I would rather own a business with:

- repeat customers;
- modest growth;
- good cash conversion;
- limited concentration; and
- manageable capital needs

than a faster-growing business whose economics depend on continued exceptional execution.

A useful question is:

> **How much can go wrong before this stops being a good business?**

The wider that margin, the higher the business quality.

---

## Rules of Thumb

- Prefer repeatability over one-off wins.
- Treat concentration on either side of the value chain as real risk.
- Ask why margins exist and why they should persist.
- Separate growth from durability.
- Look for cash conversion, not just EBITDA.
- A business that depends on one extraordinary person is not fully transferable.
- Operational improvement should create upside, not rescue the acquisition thesis.
- A good business should tolerate some mistakes by a new owner.

---

## Example: Spa Parts

Spa Parts illustrates how strengths and risks can coexist.

Positive characteristics included:

- long operating history;
- fragmented customers;
- repeat purchasing;
- historical profitability;
- low capital intensity; and
- valuable vendor relationships.

But the Balboa relationship was both:

- a strategic advantage, because approved access was difficult to replicate; and
- a major risk, because a large portion of the product base and profit depended on that supplier.

The lesson is not "supplier relationships are good" or "supplier concentration is bad."

It is:

> **Understand which characteristic actually produces value, and then ask what happens if that characteristic changes.**

---

## Failure Modes

### Confusing Growth With Quality

A fast-growing business can still have weak retention, poor cash conversion, or fragile economics.

### Treating Historical Relationships as Permanent

Long tenure does not guarantee that a customer, employee, or supplier will stay after a sale.

### Looking Only at Customer Concentration

Supplier, platform, channel, employee, or founder concentration can be equally important.

### Believing Every Improvement Opportunity

A CIM with ten "easy wins" may actually describe an under-managed company that requires significant work.

### Evaluating the Company Before the Acquisition

Debt and ownership transition can turn a historically healthy business into a fragile one.

---

## Open Questions

- How should I quantify durability across different business models?
- When does repeat purchasing become sufficiently predictable to underwrite like recurring revenue?
- How much owner dependence is acceptable when the seller supports a long transition?
- When is complexity a moat rather than simply operating burden?
- How much slower growth should I tolerate in exchange for substantially better durability?
