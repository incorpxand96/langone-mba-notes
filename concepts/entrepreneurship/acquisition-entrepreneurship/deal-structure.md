# Deal Structure as Risk Allocation

## Definition

Deal structure determines **who provides capital, who receives cash, when payments occur, who retains ownership, and who bears uncertainty after closing**.

Price answers:

> What is the business worth?

Structure answers:

> Who takes which risks in paying that price?

---

## Why It Matters

Two transactions with the same enterprise value can have very different:

- cash at close;
- leverage;
- debt service;
- seller incentives;
- buyer ownership;
- downside protection; and
- post-close flexibility.

The economics of an acquisition therefore cannot be understood from purchase price alone.

---

## Framework

Five major levers recur in ETA transactions.

### 1. Senior Debt

Third-party debt finances the acquisition but creates mandatory payments.

It improves equity efficiency while reducing margin for error.

### 2. Seller Note

The seller defers part of the purchase price and becomes a creditor.

This reduces cash needed at close but creates future fixed obligations.

### 3. Investor Equity

Outside investors fund the acquisition in exchange for ownership.

Equity absorbs risk but dilutes the buyer and introduces governance and return expectations.

### 4. Seller Equity Rollover

The seller remains an owner.

This can reduce cash at close while preserving seller exposure to future upside and downside.

### 5. Earn-Out

Part of the consideration is paid only if future performance meets agreed conditions.

Earn-outs can bridge genuine uncertainty but can also create post-close conflict.

---

## Risk-Allocation Lens

Different problems call for different instruments.

### Future Performance Is Uncertain

Potential response:

- earn-out.

### Seller Wants a High Headline Price

Potential responses:

- seller note;
- rollover;
- contingent consideration; or
- lower cash at close.

### Buyer Wants Seller Alignment

Potential response:

- seller rollover.

### Company Cannot Safely Service More Debt

Potential responses:

- more equity;
- lower purchase price;
- less debt; or
- more contingent consideration.

The instrument should solve the actual disagreement.

---

## Incentives

Structure changes behavior.

A seller holding:

- **a note** wants to be repaid;
- **rollover equity** wants long-term enterprise value;
- **an earn-out** wants the specified performance metric to be maximized.

Those are not the same incentives.

The best structure should be evaluated behaviorally as well as financially.

---

## How I Think About It

I think of deal structure as a way to make uncertainty explicit.

If the buyer and seller disagree about something important, structure can sometimes answer:

> **Who gets paid if the optimistic case proves correct, and who bears the downside if it does not?**

That is more useful than treating every disagreement as a fight over headline price.

But structure has limits.

A complicated structure cannot turn a fundamentally bad business or an excessive valuation into a good acquisition.

---

## Rules of Thumb

- Price and structure are separate questions.
- Cash at close is not enterprise value.
- Seller notes are debt.
- Seller rollover is equity.
- Earn-outs are contingent consideration.
- Model all fixed obligations under a downside case.
- Preserve liquidity after closing.
- Use rollover when alignment matters.
- Use contingent consideration when the uncertainty itself is contingent.
- Do not use complexity to hide overvaluation.

---

## Example: Rollover vs. Seller Note

Replacing $2M of seller rollover with $2M of seller financing may appear neutral because both defer $2M of cash at close.

It is not neutral.

With rollover:

- seller shares upside and downside;
- the business has no equivalent fixed repayment obligation.

With a seller note:

- seller becomes a creditor;
- repayment consumes cash;
- equity alignment is weaker.

Same face value.

Different risk.

---

## Failure Modes

### Solving Valuation With Debt

Adding seller financing because the business is otherwise too expensive.

### Ignoring Balloon Payments

Treating future refinancing needs as distant problems.

### Misaligned Earn-Outs

Creating metrics that encourage behavior neither party actually wants.

### Over-Optimization

Winning every term but damaging a seller relationship needed for transition.

### Financing to the Base Case

Leaving no room for operating underperformance.

---

## Open Questions

- When does rollover equity create more governance complexity than alignment value?
- How should earn-outs be designed when the buyer controls the post-close operating decisions?
- How much seller debt is prudent relative to senior debt?
- When should a seller note be treated as a valuation concession versus simple financing?
