# Acquisition Due Diligence

## Definition

Due diligence is the process of testing whether the business I believe I am buying is actually the business that exists.

It is not principally a checklist.

It is **thesis verification**.

---

## Why It Matters

Before diligence, the buyer has a story assembled from:

- a CIM;
- seller conversations;
- broker materials;
- financial statements;
- market research; and
- preliminary analysis.

Every part of that story contains assumptions.

Diligence should identify which assumptions matter most and determine whether the evidence supports them.

---

## Framework

The most useful three-question framework is:

1. **What am I buying?**
2. **What must remain true after I buy it?**
3. **How does my buying it change the business?**

That last question is critical.

The acquisition itself can introduce:

- debt;
- seller departure;
- employee uncertainty;
- customer reactions;
- supplier reactions;
- new management costs; and
- lower liquidity.

The company should therefore be diligenced as a **post-close system**, not merely as a historical one.

---

## Phase 1: Preliminary Diligence

Ask:

> **What must be true, or I walk away?**

Front-load the handful of questions capable of killing the thesis.

Examples:

- Are earnings real?
- Will the key supplier remain?
- Will the largest customers remain?
- Can the owner actually be replaced?
- Is critical IP owned by the business?
- Can the company support the contemplated debt?

This phase should save both money and attention.

---

## Phase 2: Confirmatory Diligence

Once existential questions look survivable, broaden the work.

Determine whether findings require:

- no action;
- a lower price;
- different structure;
- an earn-out;
- escrow;
- seller transition support;
- employee retention arrangements;
- legal protection; or
- walking away.

The correct outcome of diligence is not necessarily "close."

Walking away is a valid diligence result.

---

## Major Workstreams

### Business

- customer retention and concentration;
- revenue recurrence;
- products and services;
- competitive position;
- supplier relationships;
- market dynamics;
- sales process; and
- growth claims.

### Financial / QoE

- normalized earnings;
- proof of cash;
- revenue quality;
- add-backs;
- working capital;
- inventory;
- capex;
- debt;
- accruals; and
- liabilities.

### People & Operations

- owner role;
- key employees;
- compensation;
- retention;
- SOPs;
- systems;
- redundancy; and
- management depth.

### Legal

- entity history;
- material contracts;
- change-of-control provisions;
- litigation;
- IP;
- employment matters;
- leases; and
- regulatory exposure.

---

## How I Think About It

The goal of diligence is not certainty.

The goal is to know **which uncertainties remain, how large they are, and whether I am being compensated for them**.

A useful diligence process continually converts uncertainty into one of three things:

1. confidence;
2. mitigation; or
3. a reason to stop.

---

## Rules of Thumb

- Start from the thesis, not the data room.
- Front-load potential deal breakers.
- Translate adjectives into evidence.
- Reconcile earnings to cash.
- Treat working capital as real capital.
- Verify relationships that must survive change of control.
- Test multiple risks occurring at once.
- Maintain an explicit issue list with a proposed response for each issue.
- Re-underwrite before closing.
- Ignore sunk costs.

---

## Example: Supplier Dependence

Suppose a distributor earns a large share of gross profit from one supplier.

Diligence should not stop at:

> "They have worked together for 20 years."

The relevant questions are:

- Is the relationship contractual?
- Is it transferable?
- What happens on change of control?
- Can pricing change?
- Could the supplier sell direct?
- What does EBITDA look like if the relationship weakens?

The historical relationship is evidence.

It is not protection.

---

## Failure Modes

### Checklist Diligence

Collecting documents without connecting them to the investment thesis.

### Sunk-Cost Bias

Continuing because time and money have already been spent.

### Advisor Substitution

Letting lawyers or accountants make the commercial decision.

### Single-Risk Analysis

Testing each downside independently when the real danger is interaction among risks.

### Confirmation Bias

Using diligence to prove the deal rather than test it.
