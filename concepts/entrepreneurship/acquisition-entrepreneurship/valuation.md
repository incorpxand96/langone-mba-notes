# Valuing a Small Business Acquisition

## Definition

Valuation is the process of translating the sustainable economics and risk of a business into a price I am willing to pay.

For a small-business acquisition, the fundamental relationship is simple:

> **Enterprise Value = Normalized Earnings × Multiple**

But neither input is simple.

The difficult work is deciding what earnings are sustainable and what risk-adjusted multiple those earnings deserve.

---

## Why It Matters

A strong business can be a poor investment if purchased at the wrong price.

Valuation also determines the pressure placed on the company after closing.

The higher the price:

- the more debt or equity may be required;
- the less room there may be for operating mistakes;
- the more future improvement may already be embedded in the purchase price; and
- the more severe a downside case becomes for equity holders.

Valuation is therefore not separate from operating risk.

---

## Framework

### 1. Normalize Earnings

Before debating multiples, rebuild the earnings base.

Ask:

- Which add-backs are legitimate?
- What owner compensation must be replaced?
- Are recent results unusually strong or weak?
- What costs will exist under new ownership?
- What maintenance capex is economically necessary?
- What working-capital investment is required?

A small error in normalized EBITDA can create a large error in valuation.

### 2. Assess Business Quality

A multiple should reflect factors such as:

- recurrence;
- concentration;
- margin durability;
- growth;
- cash conversion;
- capex;
- working capital;
- management depth;
- owner dependence;
- industry quality; and
- competitive position.

### 3. Use Comparables Carefully

Comparable transactions provide an external reference, not an automatic answer.

The relevant question is not:

> "What is the market multiple?"

It is:

> "Why should this company trade at the top, middle, or bottom of the relevant range?"

### 4. Separate Current Value From Upside

I prefer to pay primarily for economics that have already been demonstrated.

Operational improvements should create additional value.

They should not be required merely to justify the entry price.

### 5. Stress the Downside

Model:

- lower revenue;
- lower margin;
- lower normalized EBITDA;
- replacement hires;
- higher working capital;
- loss of a major relationship; and
- multiple adverse events occurring together.

### 6. Overlay the Capital Structure

An enterprise value is not attractive merely because the multiple looks reasonable.

Ask whether the business can safely support the financing required to pay that price.

---

## How I Think About It

I want valuation to produce a **walk-away price**, not a rationalization for winning an auction.

A competitive process tells me what other buyers may pay.

It does not tell me what I should pay.

My strongest preference is to buy a business where:

1. current sustainable earnings justify the purchase price;
2. the capital structure survives a downside case; and
3. operational improvements create upside rather than necessity.

---

## Rules of Thumb

- Normalize EBITDA before discussing the multiple.
- Price should reflect post-close economics, not seller-era economics alone.
- Do not pay full value for improvements I still need to execute.
- Comps are inputs, not conclusions.
- A competitive auction changes the market-clearing price, not business quality.
- Re-underwrite after diligence.
- Set a walk-away price before sunk costs and competitive psychology take over.
- Leave room to be wrong.

---

## Example

If a company is offered for **$5.2M**:

- at **$1.2M EBITDA**, the implied multiple is about **4.3x**;
- at **$1.0M normalized EBITDA**, the effective multiple is **5.2x**.

The purchase price is unchanged.

The investment is not.

That is why denominator discipline matters so much.

---

## Failure Modes

### Anchoring to the CIM

Starting with the seller's EBITDA and never truly rebuilding it.

### Winning the Auction

Raising price because competitors exist rather than because the business deserves it.

### Paying for the Plan

Capitalizing future improvements as though they have already occurred.

### Ignoring Capital Structure

Assuming a reasonable enterprise multiple automatically produces a safe acquisition.

### Precision Theater

Building a highly detailed model around weak assumptions.
