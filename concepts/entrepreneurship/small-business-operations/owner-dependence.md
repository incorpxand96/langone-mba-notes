# Owner Dependence

## Definition

Owner dependence exists when a company's performance relies disproportionately on the founder or owner for critical activities, relationships, knowledge, or decisions.

The owner may be:

- chief salesperson;
- technical expert;
- relationship manager;
- pricing authority;
- recruiter;
- operator;
- culture carrier; or
- final decision-maker.

The business may be profitable while still being institutionally weak.

---

## Why It Matters

Owner dependence affects:

- business value;
- transferability;
- succession;
- acquisition risk;
- employee retention; and
- the cost of replacing the owner.

In an acquisition, it creates a simple problem:

> **The buyer is purchasing historical earnings produced by a system that may include a person who is about to leave.**

If that person's contribution is not replaceable, the historical earnings base may not transfer.

---

## Framework

Map the owner's role across five areas.

### 1. Revenue

- Which customers does the owner control?
- Does the owner source most new business?
- Are customers loyal to the company or to the person?

### 2. Operations

- Which processes require owner approval?
- What happens when the owner is absent?
- Does the owner resolve exceptions personally?

### 3. Technical Knowledge

- Is important knowledge documented?
- Can other employees perform the work?
- How long would replacement training take?

### 4. External Relationships

- Which suppliers, lenders, partners, regulators, or landlords primarily know the owner?
- Are agreements institutional or personal?

### 5. Leadership

- Does the management team make decisions?
- Do employees rely on the owner for priorities, conflict resolution, or motivation?

---

## How I Think About It

I do not think owner involvement is automatically bad.

In a small company, owner energy may be an important source of value.

The real issue is whether that value is **transferable**.

A highly involved owner is manageable if:

- responsibilities are identifiable;
- key relationships can transition;
- replacement cost is understood;
- the team can absorb some functions; and
- the seller supports a realistic transition.

It becomes dangerous when the answer to "how does this work?" is repeatedly "the owner handles it."

---

## Rules of Thumb

- Map what the owner actually does, not what the org chart says.
- Normalize earnings for replacement labor.
- Identify relationships that must transfer.
- Distinguish founder charisma from company brand.
- Document tacit knowledge before it leaves.
- Do not assume the buyer can personally absorb every founder responsibility.
- Transition support should have specific deliverables.
- Reducing owner dependence usually increases enterprise value.

---

## Example

Suppose a company reports $1.2M of EBITDA, but the owner:

- personally manages the top customers;
- negotiates major suppliers;
- acts as sales leader; and
- works 55 hours per week.

If replacing those responsibilities requires a $200K executive plus additional sales capacity, the buyer should not treat the full historical $1.2M as freely transferable earnings.

Owner dependence therefore connects directly to valuation.

---

## Failure Modes

### Assuming the Buyer Is Free Labor

Replacing a seller's job with the buyer's job does not make the economic cost disappear.

### Vague Transition Agreements

"Seller will assist for six months" is less useful than specified introductions, training, process transfer, and availability.

### Ignoring Cultural Dependence

Even where tasks transfer, employees may associate trust and identity with the founder.

### Over-Professionalizing Immediately

Removing founder-era habits too quickly can destroy relationships that were part of the company's advantage.
